\begin{abstract}
This note gives the complete derivation, with every intermediate step made explicit, of the
approximate capacity bound used in the main paper (Section 2.3): an expression for the largest
number of items $M$ a sparse $K$-of-$H$ combinatorial key-value memory can store while
remaining reliably retrievable under a given level of query corruption. The bound is
structurally the same as modern Hopfield networks' proven exponential capacity
\cite{ramsauer2020}, but is derived here for a discrete, sparse code via elementary
combinatorics and extreme value theory rather than for dense continuous patterns via an
energy-function argument. We include a full, self-contained proof of the one non-elementary
step (the expected maximum of many correlated-in-effect-but-treated-as-independent Gaussian
similarities), validate the final bound against simulation, and discuss its relationship to
the wider family of capacity theorems it belongs to. We then go further than that first-moment
approximation: Section~\ref{sec:exacttail} derives an exact, non-asymptotic failure probability
directly from the hypergeometric distribution, and a corresponding rigorous sufficient bound on
capacity at any chosen target reliability $\delta$, correcting a subtle correlation error that a
natural-looking marginalization over query corruption would otherwise introduce. This exact
bound is, as expected, considerably more conservative than the first-moment approximation, and
is shown to be tight in the well-separated regime and honestly, quantifiably loose only as the
system approaches its collapse transition.
\end{abstract}

\section{Setup}

We store $M$ items. Item $m$'s key is a binary vector $h_m \in \{0,1\}^H$ with exactly $K$
active (value-$1$) coordinates -- a $K$-of-$H$ sparse code. Throughout this note we model the
$M$ stored keys as independent, uniformly random $K$-subsets of $[H] = \{1,\dots,H\}$: an
idealization relative to the true architecture, where keys are $W_{SH}$-projections of
mutually correlated combinatorial structure (solved Sudoku grids), addressed explicitly in
Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

A query is formed from the true key $h_{\text{true}}$ by flipping a fraction $\eta \in [0,1]$
of all $H$ coordinates, chosen uniformly at random without replacement -- the noise model used
throughout the main paper. Retrieval compares the query against all $M$ stored keys via
similarity (shared active coordinates) and should return the index of $h_{\text{true}}$.
Retrieval \emph{fails} when some other stored key's similarity to the query exceeds
$h_{\text{true}}$'s.

We ask: for fixed $H$, $K$, and corruption level $\eta$, how large can $M$ be before this kind
of failure becomes likely?

\section{Step 1: Similarity between two independent random keys}
\label{sec:step1}

\begin{lemma}[Hypergeometric overlap]
\label{lem:hypergeom}
Let $A, B \subset [H]$ be independent uniformly random subsets with $|A| = a$, $|B| = b$. Then
$|A \cap B|$ is a hypergeometric random variable with
\[
\mathbb{E}[|A \cap B|] = \frac{ab}{H}, \qquad
\mathrm{Var}(|A \cap B|) = a\cdot\frac{b}{H}\Big(1-\frac{b}{H}\Big)\cdot\frac{H-a}{H-1}.
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
Fix $A$ and treat $B$'s $b$ elements as drawn sequentially without replacement from $[H]$.
Let $X_i = \mathbb{1}[\text{the $i$-th draw lands in } A]$, so $|A\cap B| = \sum_{i=1}^b X_i$.
Each $X_i$ is Bernoulli$(a/H)$ by symmetry, giving the mean by linearity of expectation. For
the variance, $\mathrm{Cov}(X_i,X_j) < 0$ for $i\ne j$ because sampling without replacement
makes draws negatively associated (having drawn a marked element makes drawing another
marked element on a later draw slightly less likely); summing the $b$ variances and
$\binom{b}{2}$ covariances gives the stated finite-population-corrected formula. (Full
derivation: Feller, \textit{An Introduction to Probability Theory}, Vol.\ 1, Ch.\ II.)
\end{proof}

Applying Lemma~\ref{lem:hypergeom} with $a=b=K$ gives the similarity between two
\emph{uncorrupted} stored keys: mean $K^2/H$, the number quoted throughout the main paper (e.g.\
$K^2/H = 22.5$ for the default $H=1000$, $K=150$).

\section{Step 2: Statistics of a corrupted query}
\label{sec:step2}

Flipping a uniformly random fraction $\eta$ of all $H$ coordinates is itself an instance of
Lemma~\ref{lem:hypergeom}: the $\eta H$ flipped positions are a random subset of $[H]$, and we
ask how many of $h_{\text{true}}$'s $K$ active positions they contain. By the lemma (with
$a=K$, $b=\eta H$), the expected number of active positions flipped \emph{off} is $\eta K$, so
\begin{equation}
S_{\text{true}}(\eta) \;=\; \mathbb{E}\big[\,\text{similarity of query to } h_{\text{true}}\,\big] \;=\; K(1-\eta),
\label{eq:strue}
\end{equation}
exactly (no further approximation needed: this is a direct expectation, not a bound). The
expected number of originally-\emph{inactive} positions flipped \emph{on} is, symmetrically,
$\eta(H-K)$, so the corrupted query's own total active-coordinate count is, in expectation,
\begin{equation}
K_q(\eta) \;=\; K(1-\eta) + \eta(H-K) \;=\; K(1-2\eta) + \eta H.
\label{eq:kq}
\end{equation}
The query's similarity to any \emph{other}, independent stored key is now Lemma~\ref{lem:hypergeom}
applied with $a = K_q(\eta)$, $b=K$:
\begin{equation}
\mu_{\text{other}}(\eta) = \frac{K_q(\eta)\,K}{H}, \qquad
\sigma_{\text{other}}^2(\eta) = K_q(\eta)\,\frac{K}{H}\Big(1-\frac{K}{H}\Big)\frac{H-K_q(\eta)}{H-1}.
\label{eq:muother}
\end{equation}

\section{Step 3: Gaussian approximation}
\label{sec:step3}

The hypergeometric distribution of $S_{\text{other}}$ (similarity to one specific other key) is
exact but discrete and unwieldy for the extreme-value step that follows. We approximate
\[
S_{\text{other}} \;\approx\; \mathcal{N}\big(\mu_{\text{other}}(\eta),\, \sigma_{\text{other}}^2(\eta)\big),
\]
justified for $H \gg 1$ by the central limit theorem for sampling without replacement from a
finite population \cite{erdosrenyi1959,hajek1960}: sums arising from simple random sampling
without replacement are asymptotically normal as the population size grows, with exactly the
finite-population-corrected variance already appearing in Lemma~\ref{lem:hypergeom}. This is
the least exact step in the derivation, and its error is largest precisely when $\sigma_{\text{other}}$
is small relative to the mean -- i.e.\ at low noise, where the discreteness of the underlying
hypergeometric distribution matters most. Section~\ref{sec:validation} quantifies this error
directly rather than leaving it as a caveat.

\section{Step 4: The maximum of many competitors}
\label{sec:step4}

Retrieval does not fail when the \emph{typical} competing key looks similar to the query --
it fails when the \emph{single most similar} of the $M-1$ competitors exceeds
$S_{\text{true}}(\eta)$. This is an extreme-value criterion, and it is the step that most
distinguishes this derivation from a naive signal-to-noise argument.

\begin{lemma}[Expected maximum of i.i.d.\ Gaussians]
\label{lem:extreme}
Let $Z_1,\dots,Z_n$ be i.i.d.\ $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$. Then
\[
\mathbb{E}\Big[\max_{1\le i\le n} Z_i\Big] \;\le\; \sqrt{2\ln n} \;+\; o(1) \quad \text{as } n\to\infty,
\]
and this bound is asymptotically tight: $\mathbb{E}[\max_i Z_i] = \sqrt{2\ln n}\,(1+o(1))$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}[Proof of the upper bound]
For any $t \ge 0$, the standard Gaussian tail (Chernoff) bound gives $\Pr(Z_1 > t) \le
e^{-t^2/2}$: with $s=t$, $\Pr(Z_1>t) = \Pr(e^{sZ_1} > e^{st}) \le \mathbb{E}[e^{sZ_1}]e^{-st} =
e^{s^2/2 - st}$, minimized at $s=t$. A union bound over the $n$ variables gives $\Pr(\max_i Z_i
> t) \le \min(1,\, n e^{-t^2/2})$. Writing the expectation of a nonnegative random variable as
an integral of its tail,
\[
\mathbb{E}\Big[\max_i Z_i\Big] = \int_0^\infty \Pr\Big(\max_i Z_i > t\Big)\,dt
\;\le\; \int_0^{t^*} 1 \,dt + \int_{t^*}^\infty n e^{-t^2/2}\,dt,
\]
where $t^* = \sqrt{2\ln n}$ is chosen so the two bounds on the integrand meet ($n e^{-(t^*)^2/2}
= 1$). The first integral is exactly $t^* = \sqrt{2\ln n}$. For the second, the standard
Gaussian tail estimate $\int_a^\infty e^{-t^2/2}\,dt \le \tfrac{1}{a}e^{-a^2/2}$ (for $a>0$,
obtained by writing $e^{-t^2/2} \le \tfrac{t}{a}e^{-t^2/2}$ on $t\ge a$ and integrating exactly)
gives
\[
\int_{t^*}^\infty n e^{-t^2/2}\,dt \;\le\; \frac{n}{t^*}e^{-(t^*)^2/2} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\ln n}} \;\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}\; 0.
\]
Summing the two pieces gives $\mathbb{E}[\max_i Z_i] \le \sqrt{2\ln n} + o(1)$. The matching
lower bound requires a second-moment (Paley--Zygmund-type) argument and is omitted here; see
Boucheron, Lugosi \& Massart, \textit{Concentration Inequalities} \cite{blm2013}, Ch.\ 2, for
the complete two-sided result.
\end{proof}

Rescaling Lemma~\ref{lem:extreme} to $\mathcal{N}(\mu_{\text{other}},\sigma_{\text{other}}^2)$
variables and setting $n = M-1$ (the number of competing stored keys) gives the working
approximation used in the main paper:
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{E}\Big[\max_{i=1}^{M-1} S_{\text{other},i}\Big] \;\approx\; \mu_{\text{other}}(\eta) + \sigma_{\text{other}}(\eta)\sqrt{2\ln(M-1)}.
\label{eq:expmax}
\end{equation}

\section{Step 5: Solving for capacity}
\label{sec:step5}

Retrieval is (approximately, at the level of expectations rather than a proper tail bound on
failure probability) reliable when $S_{\text{true}}(\eta)$ exceeds the expected maximum
competitor, Equation~\ref{eq:expmax}. Define the normalized separation margin
\begin{equation}
z(\eta) \;=\; \frac{S_{\text{true}}(\eta) - \mu_{\text{other}}(\eta)}{\sigma_{\text{other}}(\eta)}.
\label{eq:margin}
\end{equation}
Setting $S_{\text{true}}(\eta) = \mu_{\text{other}}(\eta) + \sigma_{\text{other}}(\eta)\sqrt{2\ln(M-1)}$
and solving for $M$:
\begin{equation}
z(\eta) = \sqrt{2\ln(M-1)} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad M-1 = \exp\!\left(\frac{z(\eta)^2}{2}\right)
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\boxed{M_{\max}(\eta) \;\approx\; 1 + \exp\!\left(\frac{z(\eta)^2}{2}\right).}
\label{eq:mmax}
\end{equation}

\begin{proposition}[Structural form]
Equation~\ref{eq:mmax} states that capacity is exponential in the \emph{square} of a
normalized signal-to-noise-like quantity. This is the same structural form as modern Hopfield
networks' proven capacity $C \cong 2^{d/2}$ \cite{ramsauer2020}, though that bound is derived
via a Lyapunov energy-function argument (the Concave--Convex Procedure applied to a
log-sum-exp energy) over dense continuous patterns, not via hypergeometric statistics over a
sparse discrete code.
\end{proposition}

\section{An exact hypergeometric tail bound}
\label{sec:exacttail}

Equation~\ref{eq:mmax} matches only the \emph{expected} maximum competitor similarity, not a
probabilistic guarantee on retrieval failure -- a gap noted below in
Section~\ref{sec:limitations}. This section closes it with an exact, non-asymptotic tail bound
on the failure probability itself, derived directly from the hypergeometric distribution rather
than its Gaussian approximation, and valid at any finite $M$.

\subsection{A correlation a naive marginalization misses}
\label{sec:exacttail-pitfall}

Let $R$ denote the (random) number of $h_{\text{true}}$'s $K$ active positions that land among
the $\eta H$ flipped query positions. By Lemma~\ref{lem:hypergeom} (with $a=K$, $b=\eta H$), $R$
is exactly hypergeometric:
\[
R \sim \mathrm{Hypergeometric}(H,K,\eta H), \qquad
\Pr(R=r) = \frac{\binom{K}{r}\binom{H-K}{\eta H - r}}{\binom{H}{\eta H}}.
\]
Conditional on $R=r$, both the query's active-coordinate count and its similarity to
$h_{\text{true}}$ are \emph{deterministic}, not merely expected:
\[
k(r) = K + \eta H - 2r, \qquad s(r) = K - r,
\]
the realized versions of Equations~\ref{eq:kq} and \ref{eq:strue}, which recover
$k(\eta K)=K_q(\eta)$ and $s(\eta K)=S_{\text{true}}(\eta)$ only in expectation. Crucially,
$k(r)$ and $s(r)$ are perfectly correlated through the same draw of $r$: a query realization
with more of its true bits flipped off (large $r$) simultaneously has lower true-similarity
\emph{and} a different active count. A natural-looking but invalid shortcut is to hold $s(r)$
fixed at its unconditional expectation $S_{\text{true}}(\eta)=K(1-\eta)$ while still letting
$k(r)$ vary with $r$ inside the union bound over competitors: since $k(r)$ and $s(r)$ are
deterministic functions of the very same random variable, fixing one while randomizing the
other breaks their correlation and is not a valid marginalization of any well-defined random
process. Numerically, this shortcut understates the true failure probability by roughly four to
five orders of magnitude for $\eta \in [0.30, 0.40]$, converging to the correct value only as
$\eta$ approaches the collapse region (where $r$'s distribution concentrates and the distinction
between fixing $s(r)$ at $r$ or at $\mathbb{E}[r]$ stops mattering).

\subsection{Exact per-realization failure probability}

Fix one specific other stored key $h_j$ ($j \ne \text{true}$), itself an independent random
$K$-subset of $[H]$. Conditional on $R=r$, the query is a specific, realized $k(r)$-subset, and
its overlap with $h_j$ is exactly hypergeometric by Lemma~\ref{lem:hypergeom} (with $a=k(r)$,
$b=K$). Define the exact tail probability
\[
p_k(s) \;:=\; \Pr\big[\mathrm{Hypergeometric}(H,k,K) \ge s\big]
\;=\; \sum_{i=s}^{\min(k,K)} \frac{\binom{k}{i}\binom{H-k}{K-i}}{\binom{H}{K}}.
\]
(We use $\ge$ rather than strict $>$: an exact tie is conservatively counted as a failure, which
can only inflate the resulting probability and keeps Proposition~\ref{prop:corrected} below a
genuine upper bound regardless of how ties are broken at retrieval time.) Conditional on $R=r$,
the probability that this one specific competitor's similarity meets or exceeds
$h_{\text{true}}$'s is exactly $p_{k(r)}(s(r))$, and with $M-1$ independent competitors the
conditional failure probability is exactly
\[
\Pr[\text{fail} \mid R=r] \;=\; 1 - \big[1-p_{k(r)}(s(r))\big]^{M-1}.
\]
Averaging over $R$ gives the exact unconditional failure probability -- no Gaussian
approximation, no expectation-of-a-maximum heuristic, and no fixing of $s(r)$ at its mean:
\begin{equation}
P_{\text{fail}}(M,\eta) \;=\; \sum_{r=0}^{K} \Pr(R=r) \cdot
\Big\{ 1 - \big[1-p_{k(r)}(s(r))\big]^{M-1} \Big\}.
\label{eq:exactfail}
\end{equation}
Equation~\ref{eq:exactfail} is exact given the independence assumption of
Section~\ref{sec:limitations}; it involves only a finite sum over $r \in \{0,\dots,K\}$ and is
cheap to evaluate for any $H,K,\eta,M$.

\noindent\textbf{A small-tail approximation and a characteristic scale.} Away from the collapse
transition, $p_{k(r)}(s(r))$ is typically small, and
\[
\big[1-p_{k(r)}(s(r))\big]^{M-1} \;\approx\; \exp\big[-(M-1)\,p_{k(r)}(s(r))\big],
\]
so, conditional on $R=r$, retrieval probability decays approximately exponentially in $M$ with a
characteristic scale
\[
M^*_r \;\sim\; \frac{1}{p_{k(r)}(s(r))}.
\]
Intuitively: a dangerous competitor is individually unlikely at any single comparison, but there
are $M-1$ near-independent opportunities for one to occur, and their cumulative probability grows
with memory load -- the same ``many rare independent chances'' structure behind a Poisson
approximation, applied here to the union of $M-1$ competitor comparisons. This is an approximation
used only for intuition; Equation~\ref{eq:exactfail} itself requires no such approximation.

\subsection{No finite-size singularity: monotonicity and convexity in $M$}
\label{sec:smoothness}

Equation~\ref{eq:exactfail} is not just numerically well-behaved (Section~\ref{sec:exacttail-validation}
below) -- it is provably free of any finite-$M$ singularity of the kind a genuine attractor-collapse
memory cliff would produce. This is a structural property of the exact formula itself, independent of
the union bound derived in the next section, and is worth stating as a theorem in its own right.

\begin{theorem}[No finite-size singularity]
\label{thm:smoothness}
Fix $H$, $K$, and $\eta$, and suppose $0 < p_{k(r)}(s(r)) < 1$ for every $r$ with $\Pr(R=r)>0$ (against
any realized corruption level, a single competitor's failure is neither impossible nor certain).
Extend Equation~\ref{eq:exactfail} to continuous $M \ge 1$ via
\[
P_{\mathrm{ret}}(M,\eta) \;:=\; 1 - P_{\mathrm{fail}}(M,\eta) \;=\; \sum_{r=0}^{K} \Pr(R=r)\,
\big[1-p_{k(r)}(s(r))\big]^{M-1}.
\]
Then $P_{\mathrm{ret}}(M,\eta)$ is strictly decreasing and strictly convex in $M$:
\[
\frac{\partial P_{\mathrm{ret}}}{\partial M} < 0, \qquad \frac{\partial^2 P_{\mathrm{ret}}}{\partial M^2} > 0.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Write $q_r := 1-p_{k(r)}(s(r)) \in (0,1)$ for each $r$ with $\Pr(R=r)>0$. Treating $M$ as a continuous
variable, $q_r^{M-1} = \exp[(M-1)\ln q_r]$, so
\[
\frac{\partial}{\partial M}\,q_r^{M-1} = q_r^{M-1}\ln q_r,
\qquad
\frac{\partial^2}{\partial M^2}\,q_r^{M-1} = q_r^{M-1}(\ln q_r)^2.
\]
Since $q_r \in (0,1)$, $\ln q_r < 0$ and $q_r^{M-1}>0$, so the first derivative is strictly negative
and the second, a square of a nonzero real number times a positive factor, is strictly positive.
$P_{\mathrm{ret}}(M,\eta)$ is a finite sum of these terms weighted by $\Pr(R=r)\ge0$ summing to $1$;
differentiation is linear and commutes with a finite sum, so
\[
\frac{\partial P_{\mathrm{ret}}}{\partial M} = \sum_{r} \Pr(R=r)\,q_r^{M-1}\ln q_r \;<\; 0,
\qquad
\frac{\partial^2 P_{\mathrm{ret}}}{\partial M^2} = \sum_{r} \Pr(R=r)\,q_r^{M-1}(\ln q_r)^2 \;>\; 0,
\]
each a weighted average, with at least one strictly positive weight, of terms that are all strictly
negative (respectively all strictly positive).
\end{proof}

\noindent\textbf{Interpretation.} The precise claim is \emph{no finite-$M$ singularity}, not
\emph{no cliff} in every informal sense of the word: Theorem~\ref{thm:smoothness} rules out, at the
level of the exact model, any finite-$M$ point at which retrieval probability drops
discontinuously or bends toward an accelerating collapse -- $P_{\mathrm{ret}}$ can only
\emph{decelerate} as $M$ grows (convexity), on top of always decreasing (monotonicity). It does
not, by itself, rule out $P_{\mathrm{ret}}$ falling steeply over a narrow range of $M$ while
remaining continuous and convex throughout; Section~\ref{sec:outlook} shows such steepness can in
fact become asymptotically sharp under exponential-in-$H$ storage. What is proven here is the
absence of a true discontinuity or accelerating (non-convex) collapse at any finite size, now
established rather than only observed in simulation (Section~\ref{sec:exacttail-validation}). This is a property specific
to the \emph{explicit-key} retrieval mechanism analyzed throughout this note: failure accumulates from
more chances for some one unrelated stored key to accidentally resemble the query, not from the
appearance or disappearance of a dynamical attractor -- the latter being the mechanism behind a
genuine finite-size memory cliff in attractor-based (Hopfield-type) associative memories, and a
distinction this note does not claim to resolve for that broader model class (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\subsection{A rigorous, closed-form sufficient bound}

Equation~\ref{eq:exactfail} is exact but implicit: it gives $P_{\text{fail}}$ as a function of
$M$, not $M$ as a function of a target failure probability $\delta$. We invert it via the union
bound, applied \emph{per realization of $r$} before averaging -- the order matters, and doing it
in this order is what keeps the resulting bound valid.

By Bernoulli's inequality, $1-(1-p)^n \le np$ for any $p\in[0,1]$ and integer $n \ge 0$. Applied
pointwise, for every $r$,
\[
1 - \big[1-p_{k(r)}(s(r))\big]^{M-1} \;\le\; (M-1)\,p_{k(r)}(s(r)).
\]
This is a deterministic inequality between two functions of $r$; taking $\mathbb{E}_R[\cdot]$ of
both sides preserves it, since expectation is monotone:
\begin{equation}
P_{\text{fail}}(M,\eta) \;\le\; (M-1) \cdot
\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_R\big[\,p_{k(R)}(s(R))\,\big]}_{=:\ \bar P(\eta)}.
\label{eq:pbar}
\end{equation}

\begin{proposition}[Corrected sufficient capacity bound]
\label{prop:corrected}
For any target failure probability $\delta \in (0,1)$, storing
\begin{equation}
M \;\le\; M_{\max}(\eta,\delta) \;:=\; 1 + \left\lfloor \frac{\delta}{\bar P(\eta)} \right\rfloor
\label{eq:mmax-exact}
\end{equation}
items guarantees $P_{\text{fail}}(M,\eta) \le \delta$ exactly, subject only to the independence
assumption of Section~\ref{sec:limitations} -- not to any Gaussian or large-$M$ asymptotic
approximation.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Immediate from Equation~\ref{eq:pbar}: $(M-1)\bar P(\eta) \le \delta$ whenever
$M - 1 \le \delta/\bar P(\eta)$, i.e.\ $M \le 1 + \lfloor \delta/\bar P(\eta) \rfloor$.
\end{proof}

Equation~\ref{eq:mmax-exact} has the same union-bound shape as a natural first attempt at this
formula, $1+\lfloor \delta/p_{K_q(\eta)}(S_{\text{true}}(\eta))\rfloor$, but with $p$ correctly
taken to be $\bar P(\eta) = \mathbb{E}_R[p_{k(R)}(s(R))]$ -- the per-realization tail probability
\emph{properly averaged} over $R$ -- rather than a single tail probability evaluated at the
mismatched pair $\big(K_q(\eta), S_{\text{true}}(\eta)\big)$, which (Section~\ref{sec:exacttail-pitfall})
treats the numerator and denominator of the same random draw as independently settable.

starkly than that hedge anticipated.

silently.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

Two assumptions are false by construction and are stated as such rather than absorbed silently.

\textbf{Independence.} Real stored keys are $W_{SH}$-projections of genuinely correlated
combinatorial objects (solved Sudoku grids share row/column/box structure), not independent
uniform random subsets. The observed collapse thresholds sit consistently at or slightly above
the predicted ones (Section~\ref{sec:validation}); a plausible explanation is that this real
correlation makes the worst-case competing key marginally less extreme than the independent
model assumes, though this has not been tested directly.

\textbf{The extreme-value step is a first-moment approximation, not a tail bound -- now
resolved.} Equation \ref{eq:expmax} matches the \emph{expected} maximum, not a high-probability
bound on it. Section~\ref{sec:exacttail} closes this gap with an exact, non-asymptotic
alternative (Equation~\ref{eq:exactfail}) and a corresponding rigorous sufficient bound
(Proposition~\ref{prop:corrected}), derived directly from the hypergeometric distribution rather
than the Gaussian approximation or the expected-maximum heuristic. As anticipated, the rigorous
bound gives a substantially smaller $M_{\max}$ at any fixed target failure probability --
by several orders of magnitude away from the collapse region (Table~\ref{tab:exacttail}) -- and
is itself provably tight in that same well-separated regime, growing conservatively (rather than
silently failing) only as $\eta$ approaches collapse (Table~\ref{tab:tightness}). Equation
\ref{eq:mmax} should now be read as characterizing the right \emph{mechanism} -- exponential
capacity in the squared separation margin, hence a sharp rather than gradual collapse -- while
Equation~\ref{eq:mmax-exact} is the bound to use for an actual reliability guarantee at a chosen
$\delta$. Both bounds still share the independence assumption below, which remains open.

\section{An asymptotic large-deviation phase boundary}
\label{sec:outlook}

Theorem~\ref{thm:smoothness} rules out a finite-$M$ singularity at fixed $H$. It does not rule out
an asymptotically sharp threshold if $H$ and $M$ are taken to infinity together in a suitable
exponential scaling regime -- the same sense in which Shannon's channel coding theorem
\cite{coverthomas} produces a sharp rate/capacity boundary only in the block-length limit, never at
any finite block length. This section derives that rate function explicitly from the hypergeometric
tail, closing the gap an earlier version of this note left open.

\subsection{Setup}

Let $\rho=K/H$ and, for a query corrupted at level $\eta$, work at the typical corruption level
$r=\eta K$ (the mean of $R\sim\mathrm{Hypergeometric}(H,K,\eta H)$), giving the typical query
activity $\kappa H := k(\eta K) = \rho(1-2\eta)H+\eta H$ and typical true similarity
$xH := s(\eta K) = \rho(1-\eta)H$. The per-competitor dangerous-tail probability is then
$p_H(\eta,\rho) \approx \Pr[\mathrm{Hypergeometric}(H,\kappa H,\rho H)\ge xH]$, and since $x>\kappa\rho$
(the target lies above the hypergeometric mean $\kappa\rho H$, exactly the extreme-tail regime a
large-deviation principle governs), we seek its exponential decay rate.

\begin{proposition}[Large-deviation rate function for the hypergeometric tail]
\label{prop:ratefunction}
Let $h(p):=-p\ln p-(1-p)\ln(1-p)$ (natural-log binary entropy). As $H\to\infty$ with $\rho,\kappa,x$
fixed,
\[
-\frac{1}{H}\ln \Pr\big[\mathrm{Hypergeometric}(H,\kappa H,\rho H)\ge xH\big] \;\longrightarrow\; I(x;\kappa,\rho), \qquad\text{where}
\]
\[
I(x;\kappa,\rho) \;:=\; h(\rho) \;-\; \kappa\,h\!\left(\frac{x}{\kappa}\right) \;-\; (1-\kappa)\,h\!\left(\frac{\rho-x}{1-\kappa}\right).
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Write the hypergeometric PMF as $\Pr[X=a] = \binom{\kappa H}{a}\binom{(1-\kappa)H}{\rho H-a}/\binom{H}{\rho H}$.
Stirling's approximation gives $\ln\binom{n}{m}=nh(m/n)+O(\ln n)$, so with $a=xH$,
\[
\frac{1}{H}\ln\Pr[X=xH] \;=\; \kappa\,h\!\left(\frac{x}{\kappa}\right) + (1-\kappa)\,h\!\left(\frac{\rho-x}{1-\kappa}\right) - h(\rho) + O\!\left(\frac{\ln H}{H}\right).
\]
Since $x>\kappa\rho$ lies above the mean and the hypergeometric PMF is log-concave (unimodal), the
tail sum $\Pr[X\ge xH]=\sum_{a'\ge xH}\Pr[X=a']$ is dominated to leading exponential order by its
smallest-rate (boundary) term, so $-\frac1H\ln\Pr[X\ge xH]$ has the same limit as
$-\frac1H\ln\Pr[X=xH]$, giving $I(x;\kappa,\rho)$ as stated (the number of terms summed is only
polynomial in $H$ and does not affect the exponential rate).
\end{proof}

\subsection{Why substituting the typical corruption realization is not enough}
\label{sec:typical-r-pitfall}

Substituting the \emph{typical} corruption level $r=\eta K$ into $\kappa=\rho(1-2\eta)+\eta$,
$x=\rho(1-\eta)$ gives a candidate rate
\begin{equation}
I_{\mathrm{typical}}(\eta,\rho) \;:=\; h(\rho) - \kappa\,h\!\left(\frac{\rho(1-\eta)}{\kappa}\right) - (1-\kappa)\,h\!\left(\frac{\rho\eta}{1-\kappa}\right), \qquad \kappa=\rho(1-2\eta)+\eta,
\label{eq:rate-typical}
\end{equation}
which an earlier version of this note used directly as $I(\eta,\rho)$. This is the same error
Section~\ref{sec:exacttail-pitfall} already diagnosed and corrected at the level of the exact,
finite-$H$ formula: $R$ is itself random, and $\kappa$, $x$ are both deterministic functions of
the \emph{same} realization of $R$, not independently free to be set at their unconditional means.
At the level of a large-deviation \emph{rate}, this substitution is not merely imprecise but
asymptotically wrong: it ignores that an \emph{atypical} realization of $R$ -- individually rare,
at its own exponential cost -- can make the resulting tail event cheaper overall than the typical
realization does, and the true asymptotic rate is governed by the cheapest such path, not the
typical one. Since atypical paths can only lower the total cost, $I_{\mathrm{typical}}(\eta,\rho)$
is provably an \emph{overestimate} of the true rate -- optimistic, not conservative, about how much
corruption can be tolerated.

\subsection{The correct rate: a large-deviation contraction over the corruption realization}
\label{sec:corrected-rate}

$R\sim\mathrm{Hypergeometric}(H,K,\eta H)$ obeys the same large-deviation principle as
Proposition~\ref{prop:ratefunction}, applied to $R$ itself: writing $x_R:=r/H$, population fraction
$\rho$ (true-active bits) and sample fraction $\eta$ (flipped positions),
\[
-\tfrac1H\ln\Pr[R=x_R H] \;\longrightarrow\; I_R(x_R) \;:=\; I(x_R;\rho,\eta).
\]
Conditional on a realized $x_R$, the tail rate from Proposition~\ref{prop:ratefunction} (now with
the \emph{realized}, not typical, query activity $\kappa(x_R)=\rho+\eta-2x_R$ and true similarity
$x(x_R)=\rho-x_R$) is $I_{\mathrm{tail}}(x_R) := I\big(\rho-x_R;\ \rho+\eta-2x_R,\ \rho\big)$.

\begin{proposition}[Corrected large-deviation rate, via contraction]
\label{prop:corrected-rate}
\[
I(\eta,\rho) \;:=\; \min_{x_R \in [\max(0,\rho+\eta-1),\ \min(\rho,\eta)]} \Big[\, I_R(x_R) + I_{\mathrm{tail}}(x_R) \,\Big]
\]
is the correct asymptotic rate: $-\tfrac1H\ln\bar P(\eta) \to I(\eta,\rho)$ as $H\to\infty$, where
$\bar P(\eta)=\mathbb{E}_R\big[p_{k(R)}(s(R))\big]$ is the exact, already-correctly-averaged
quantity of Section~\ref{sec:exacttail-pitfall}. Since $I_R(x_R)\ge0$ everywhere and
$I_R(\rho\eta)=0$ at the typical value, $I(\eta,\rho) \le I_{\mathrm{typical}}(\eta,\rho)$ always,
with equality only if the typical realization happens to minimize the bracketed sum.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Justification]
This is the standard large-deviations contraction/Varadhan argument. Recall
\[
\bar P(\eta) \;=\; \sum_r \Pr(R=r)\,p_{k(r)}(s(r))
\]
is a sum of $H{+}1$ exponentially-scaled terms, each
$\approx\exp\{-H[I_R(x_R)+I_{\mathrm{tail}}(x_R)]\}$. A sum of exponentially many
exponentially-scaled terms is dominated, to leading exponential order, by its largest (cheapest)
term -- the $x_R$ minimizing the bracketed total cost -- since the number of terms summed is only
polynomial in $H$ and does not affect the exponential rate, exactly as in the proof of
Proposition~\ref{prop:ratefunction}.
\end{proof}

We verified Proposition~\ref{prop:corrected-rate} directly by computing the exact $\bar P(\eta)$
(via \texttt{scipy.stats.\allowbreak hypergeom}, summing over all realizable $r$ exactly, with no
approximation) at $\rho=0.15$ across a $250\times$ range of $H$. The exact rate
$-\tfrac1H\ln\bar P(\eta)$ converges cleanly to the corrected $I(\eta,\rho)$ and \emph{away from}
$I_{\mathrm{typical}}(\eta,\rho)$:

\midrule
$-\tfrac1H\ln\bar P(0.30)$ & $0.03417$ & $0.03139$ & $0.03044$ & $0.03014$ & $0.03005$ \\

\end{center}
\noindent converging to the corrected $I(0.30,0.15)=0.03001$ -- not to
$I_{\mathrm{typical}}(0.30,0.15)=0.04255$, which this convergence rules out decisively. The same
pattern holds at $\eta=0.20$ ($I=0.07721$ vs.\ $I_{\mathrm{typical}}=0.10175$) and $\eta=0.40$

vacuous and both formulas coincide.

\subsection{The corrected phase boundary and critical corruption threshold}

If the number of stored memories scales exponentially with $H$, $M=\exp(\alpha H)$, the same
substitution as before, now using the corrected rate, gives
\begin{equation}
P_{\mathrm{ret}} \;\approx\; \exp\Big\{-\exp\big[H\big(\alpha-I(\eta,\rho)\big)\big]\Big\},
\label{eq:doubleexp}
\end{equation}
a sharp threshold exactly at $\alpha=I(\eta,\rho)$, and a critical corruption level
\begin{equation}
\eta_c(\alpha,\rho) \;:=\; \text{the unique root of } I(\eta,\rho)=\alpha \text{ in } \eta \in (0,\tfrac12),
\label{eq:etac}
\end{equation}
solved numerically (bisection; $I(\eta,\rho)$ decreases strictly from $I(0,\rho)=h(\rho)$ toward
$0$ as $\eta\to\tfrac12$, verified numerically at $\rho{=}0.15$):

This is not a contradiction of Theorem~\ref{thm:smoothness}: a finite-$H$ curve that is provably
smooth and convex at every finite size can still become arbitrarily sharp in a singular joint

Theorem~\ref{thm:smoothness}) and \emph{a genuine sharp asymptotic phase boundary at

randomness of the corruption realization rather than only its typical value) under
exponential-in-$H$ storage.

\section{Relation to the wider literature}

This derivation belongs to a family of results sharing one underlying question: does a correct
answer stand out against the \emph{extreme} value among exponentially or combinatorially many
near-random alternatives? The same skeleton, worked out through different technical routes,
underlies Shannon's channel coding theorem \cite{coverthomas}, the Random Energy Model in
spin-glass theory \cite{mezardmontanari2009}, Gardner's perceptron capacity
\cite{gardner1988}, and the classical Amit--Gutfreund--Sompolinsky analysis of Hopfield network
capacity \cite{ags1987}. Kanerva's original capacity analysis for sparse distributed memory
\cite{kanerva1988} is the closest existing derivation in spirit to this one -- also a sparse
binary code, also analyzed via combinatorial (there, Hamming-ball volume) arguments -- though
via a different geometric route than the hypergeometric-overlap statistics used here.

\section{Appendix: An independent derivation of MESH's heteroassociative theorems}
\label{sec:mesh-derivation}

Section 4.5 of the main paper ties an empirically measured capacity ceiling to two specific
published results, MESH's Theorems 5.1 and 5.3 \cite{sharma2022}, governing the two directions of
pseudoinverse-learned heteroassociation between the scaffold's hidden layer and an arbitrary
feature (content) layer. We derive both independently here, from elementary linear algebra, rather
than only citing them -- and show they are the same fact, $M^+M$ acting as either the identity or
a projector depending on which side of a rank comparison $M$ sits, applied to the two different
matrices ($F$, then $H$) that appear in the two heteroassociative directions.

\paragraph{Setup.} Let $H\in\mathbb{R}^{N_H\times N_{\mathrm{patts}}}$ have columns $h^\mu$,
$\mu=1,\dots,N_{\mathrm{patts}}$, the scaffold's predefined hidden-layer states, and let
$F\in\mathbb{R}^{N_F\times N_{\mathrm{patts}}}$ have columns $f^\mu$, the arbitrary content
patterns heteroassociated with them. Heteroassociative pseudoinverse learning sets
$W_{HF}=HF^+$ (feature$\to$hidden) and $W_{FH}=FH^+$ (hidden$\to$feature).

\begin{lemma}[The pseudo-inverse ``hat'' matrix is the identity or a projector]
\label{lem:pinv-hat}
Let $M\in\mathbb{R}^{a\times b}$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(a)] If $M$ has full column rank ($\mathrm{rank}(M)=b\le a$), then $M^+M = I_b$, exactly.
\item[(b)] If $M$ has full row rank ($\mathrm{rank}(M)=a\le b$), then $P:=M^+M$ is the orthogonal
projector onto the row space of $M$, with $\mathrm{rank}(P)=a$; in particular $P=I_b$ only if
$a=b$, and otherwise $P\ne I_b$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
(a) Full column rank gives the left pseudo-inverse $M^+=(M^\top M)^{-1}M^\top$ ($M^\top M$
invertible since it is $b\times b$ of rank $b$), so $M^+M = (M^\top M)^{-1}M^\top M = I_b$.
(b) Full row rank gives the right pseudo-inverse $M^+=M^\top(MM^\top)^{-1}$ ($MM^\top$ invertible
since it is $a\times a$ of rank $a$), so $P:=M^+M = M^\top(MM^\top)^{-1}M$ is symmetric
($P^\top=M^\top((MM^\top)^{-1})^\top M = P$, since $MM^\top$ is symmetric) and idempotent
($P^2=M^\top(MM^\top)^{-1}(MM^\top)(MM^\top)^{-1}M=P$), hence an orthogonal projector, with
$\mathrm{rank}(P)=\mathrm{rank}(M)=a$. If $a<b$, $P$ is a rank-$a$ projector on
$\mathbb{R}^b$ and cannot equal $I_b$ (rank $b$).
\end{proof}

\subsection{Theorem 5.1, re-derived: exact recovery when $N_{\mathrm{patts}}\le N_F$}

MESH's Theorem 5.1 states that if $F$ is full rank, an input of clean feature vectors perfectly
reconstructs the hidden layer states through pseudoinverse heteroassociation, provided
$N_{\mathrm{patts}}\le N_F$ -- exactly the regime in which $F$ (an $N_F\times N_{\mathrm{patts}}$
matrix) can have full \emph{column} rank $N_{\mathrm{patts}}$.

\begin{proposition}[MESH's Theorem 5.1, re-derived]
\label{prop:mesh-thm51}
If $F$ has full column rank $N_{\mathrm{patts}}$ (possible since $N_{\mathrm{patts}}\le N_F$),
then presenting the clean feature vector $f^\mu$ recovers the hidden state exactly:
$\hat h^\mu := W_{HF}f^\mu = h^\mu$, for every $\mu$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Writing $f^\mu=Fe_\mu$, Lemma~\ref{lem:pinv-hat}(a) applied to $F$ gives $F^+F=I_{N_{\mathrm{patts}}}$,
so
\[
\hat h^\mu = W_{HF}f^\mu = HF^+Fe_\mu = H\,I_{N_{\mathrm{patts}}}\,e_\mu = He_\mu = h^\mu.
\]
\end{proof}

Unlike Theorem 5.3 below, this is an exact deterministic identity, not an expectation: it requires
no distributional assumption on $F$ or $H$ at all beyond $F$'s rank, and holds for every stored
pattern individually, not merely on average. We verified it numerically (random Gaussian $F$ and
$H$) across four $(N_F,N_{\mathrm{patts}},N_H)$ triples spanning an order of magnitude in each
dimension; the maximum reconstruction error was at floating-point precision
($\le 2\times10^{-12}$) in every case, exactly as the exact identity predicts.

\subsection{Theorem 5.3, re-derived: partial recovery when $N_{\mathrm{patts}}>N_H$}

The complementary regime reverses which matrix supplies the rank condition and which supplies the
randomness. Assume: (i) $N_{\mathrm{patts}}>N_H$ and $H$ has full row rank $N_H$ (guaranteed for
the scaffold's own predefined states by construction \cite{sharma2022}, independent of $F$); (ii)
the columns $f^\mu$ are independent, zero-mean, and each satisfies $(f^\mu)^\top f^\mu=N_F$
exactly (e.g.\ i.i.d.\ random $\{-1,1\}$ patterns), and are independent of $H$. Presenting the
exact hidden state $h^\mu$ recovers $\hat f^\mu = W_{FH}h^\mu = FH^+h^\mu$.

\begin{proposition}[MESH's Theorem 5.3, re-derived]
\label{prop:mesh-rederived}
Under assumptions (i)--(ii),
\[
\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{patts}}}\sum_{\mu=1}^{N_{\mathrm{patts}}}
\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{(f^\mu)^\top \hat f^\mu}{N_F}\right] \;=\; \frac{N_H}{N_{\mathrm{patts}}}.
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Writing $h^\mu=He_\mu$, Lemma~\ref{lem:pinv-hat}(b) applied to $H$ gives $\hat f^\mu = FH^+He_\mu =
Fc^\mu$ where $c^\mu:=Pe_\mu$ and $P:=H^+H$ is the rank-$N_H$ orthogonal projector onto $H$'s row
space. The vector $c^\mu$ is column $\mu$ of $P$, so its own $\mu$-th entry is the diagonal
element $c^\mu_\mu=P_{\mu\mu}$. Expanding the reconstruction over the pattern basis,
\[
\hat f^\mu = \sum_{\nu=1}^{N_{\mathrm{patts}}} c^\mu_\nu f^\nu = c^\mu_\mu f^\mu + \sum_{\nu\ne\mu} c^\mu_\nu f^\nu,
\]
so $(f^\mu)^\top\hat f^\mu = c^\mu_\mu(f^\mu)^\top f^\mu + \sum_{\nu\ne\mu}c^\mu_\nu(f^\mu)^\top f^\nu
= c^\mu_\mu N_F + \sum_{\nu\ne\mu}c^\mu_\nu(f^\mu)^\top f^\nu$. Taking expectation over $F$ (with
$H$, hence $c^\mu$, independent of $F$), every cross term $\mathbb{E}[(f^\mu)^\top f^\nu]=0$ for
$\nu\ne\mu$ by assumption (ii), leaving
\[
\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{(f^\mu)^\top \hat f^\mu}{N_F}\right] = c^\mu_\mu = P_{\mu\mu}.
\]
Averaging over all $N_{\mathrm{patts}}$ patterns,
$\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{patts}}}\sum_\mu P_{\mu\mu} = \mathrm{tr}(P)/N_{\mathrm{patts}}$. $P$ is an
orthogonal projector, so its eigenvalues are all $0$ or $1$ and its trace equals its rank:
$\mathrm{tr}(P)=\mathrm{rank}(P)=N_H$. Hence the average equals $N_H/N_{\mathrm{patts}}$.
\end{proof}

We verified this numerically too (random Gaussian $H$, i.i.d.\ $\{-1,1\}$ columns for $F$) across
four $(N_H,N_{\mathrm{patts}},N_F)$ triples spanning an order of magnitude in each dimension;
measured and predicted overlap agreed to at least three decimal places in every case (e.g.\
$N_H{=}150$, $N_{\mathrm{patts}}{=}1000$: measured $0.1500$ vs.\ predicted $0.1500$).

\paragraph{Discussion.} Both theorems are corollaries of Lemma~\ref{lem:pinv-hat} applied to
different matrices. Theorem 5.1 needs $F$ full column rank ($N_{\mathrm{patts}}\le N_F$) and gets
back case (a) -- the hat matrix is the identity, so recovery is exact and deterministic, with no
distributional assumption on either $F$ or $H$ needed at all. Theorem 5.3 needs $H$ full row rank
($N_{\mathrm{patts}}>N_H$, guaranteed by the scaffold's own construction) and gets back case (b)
-- the hat matrix is a rank-deficient projector, so recovery is only partial \emph{on average},
and only under an added independence assumption on $F$ (needed here specifically to make the
projector's off-diagonal action on unrelated patterns cancel in expectation, since $P\ne I$ leaves
no exact identity to fall back on). This is also exactly why Theorem 5.3, unlike Theorem 5.1,
places no full-rank requirement on the \emph{content} matrix $F$: the ceiling is set entirely by
$H$'s rank relative to $N_{\mathrm{patts}}$. Between the two theorems, MESH's own heteroassociation
theory therefore covers both directions in which a pseudoinverse-learned heteroassociative map can
be starved of rank: too few scaffold dimensions relative to the number of patterns (Theorem 5.3),
or too little independent information in the content itself (Theorem 5.1, violated by real images
in Section 4.5 of the main paper).

\subsection{Why the same linear algebra looks catastrophic in Hopfield but graceful in MESH and
our own architecture}
\label{sec:catastrophic-vs-graceful}

Section 4.6 of the main paper reports that pseudo-inverse (projection-rule) Hopfield is
``algebraically exact at zero noise'' yet has basins of attraction that ``shrink so fast under
even modest noise'' that recall crashes from $100\%$ to $0\%$ within a narrow range of $M$. We now
show this is not a separate empirical curiosity but a direct, provable consequence of the same
pseudo-inverse machinery above, applied to \emph{auto}-association instead of heteroassociation --
and that it is this specific consequence, not the pseudo-inverse rule in the abstract, that MESH's
scaffold and our own architecture avoid.

\paragraph{Setup.} The classical projection-rule (pseudo-inverse) Hopfield weight matrix is
\[
W = X(X^\top X)^+X^\top \;\in\; \mathbb{R}^{N\times N},
\]
the orthogonal projector onto the column space of the stored-pattern matrix
$X\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times M}$ (columns $x^\mu$).

\begin{proposition}[Clean recall is always exact; noise correction is not]
\label{prop:hopfield-wall}
For every stored pattern, $Wx^\mu = x^\mu$ exactly, at any $M$. For a noisy cue
$\tilde x=x^\mu+n$, the reconstructed state is $W\tilde x = x^\mu + Wn$, and the expected fraction
of noise energy \emph{removed} by this single linear pass, for isotropic random noise $n$, is
\[
\frac{\mathbb{E}\big[\|(I-W)n\|^2\big]}{\|n\|^2} \;=\; \frac{N-\mathrm{rank}(X)}{N} \;=\; \max\!\Big(0,\ 1-\frac{M}{N}\Big),
\]
which reaches exactly zero once $M\ge N$ and remains exactly zero for every $M>N$ thereafter.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Since $x^\mu=Xe_\mu\in\mathrm{col}(X)$ trivially, and $W$ is by construction the orthogonal
projector onto $\mathrm{col}(X)$, $Wx^\mu=x^\mu$ exactly -- clean recall never degrades with $M$,
unlike heteroassociation's averaged overlap (Propositions~\ref{prop:mesh-thm51}--\ref{prop:mesh-rederived}).
For the noise term: $W$ and $I-W$ are complementary orthogonal projectors ($W(I-W)=0$), of ranks
$\mathrm{rank}(X)$ and $N-\mathrm{rank}(X)$ respectively, so by the Pythagorean identity
$\|n\|^2=\|Wn\|^2+\|(I-W)n\|^2$ for every $n$. For an isotropic random unit vector $n$, the
expected squared norm of its projection onto a fixed rank-$r$ subspace is $r/N$ (by symmetry,
each of $N$ orthogonal directions captures expected energy $1/N$); applying this to $I-W$
(rank $N-\mathrm{rank}(X)$) gives $\mathbb{E}[\|(I-W)n\|^2]=(N-\mathrm{rank}(X))/N$. Generic random
patterns give $\mathrm{rank}(X)=\min(M,N)$, so this is $\max(0,1-M/N)$, and since rank cannot
exceed $N$, it is \emph{exactly} zero, not merely small, for every $M\ge N$.
\end{proof}

We verified Proposition~\ref{prop:hopfield-wall} numerically (i.i.d.\ $\{-1,1\}$ patterns,
$N{=}500$, isotropic Gaussian noise): removed-noise fraction $0.8006$ ($M{=}100$, predicted
$0.8000$), $0.3884$ ($M{=}300$, predicted $0.4000$), and exactly $0.0000$ for every
$M\in\{500,600,1000\}$ tested, against a predicted floor of exactly $0$ once $M\ge N$.

\paragraph{The mathematical root of the asymmetry.} Auto-association's noise-correction capacity
and heteroassociation's reconstruction overlap are governed by \emph{different} projector
quantities. Auto-association's noise correction depends on $\mathrm{rank}(I-W) = N-\mathrm{rank}(X)$
-- an integer that hits zero exactly at a specific, finite $M$ and cannot go lower, because rank is
bounded below by zero. Heteroassociation's overlap (Propositions~\ref{prop:mesh-thm51}--\ref{prop:mesh-rederived})
depends instead on the \emph{average diagonal entry} of a projector, $\mathrm{tr}(P)/N_{\mathrm{patts}}=N_H/N_{\mathrm{patts}}$
-- a ratio that shrinks smoothly toward, but never exactly reaches, zero at any finite
$N_{\mathrm{patts}}$, and is provably monotone and convex in the load variable
(Theorem~\ref{thm:smoothness}, proved for our own architecture's actual capacity-bearing readout;
MESH's Theorem 4.3 establishes the analogous robustness for its own scaffold, by a different,
combinatorial argument). One is a hard, deterministic floor reached at a specific point; the other
is a ratio that only approaches its limit.

\paragraph{Where the catastrophe actually comes from.} Proposition~\ref{prop:hopfield-wall} alone
would predict only a hard-but-finite wall: at $M=N$ a single linear pass stops helping with noise
at all, but nothing forces it to get \emph{worse} beyond that point, nor explains sub-100\% recall
already at $M<N$ under noise. That amplification comes from the auto-associative network's
recurrent, nonlinear dynamics ($x\to\mathrm{sign}(Wx)$, iterated to a fixed point), whose
basin-of-attraction structure near this same $M\approx N$ boundary is known, via the classical
statistical mechanics of Hopfield networks, to undergo its own sharp transition -- the
Amit--Gutfreund--Sompolinsky storage-capacity analysis and Kanter \& Sompolinsky's projection-rule
result \cite{ags1987,kanter1987}, already cited in Section 4.6 of the main paper for exactly this
reason. We do not re-derive that transition here; what Proposition~\ref{prop:hopfield-wall}
establishes rigorously is the boundary condition feeding into it: the linear noise-correction
mechanism is provably, exactly exhausted at $M=N$ (or, for real correlated content such as the
MNIST images of Section 4.5, at the content's own effective rank rather than its nominal
dimension) -- precisely where that separately-established recurrent instability becomes
catastrophic rather than merely diminished.

\paragraph{Why MESH and our own architecture do not inherit this wall.} Neither routes an
imperfect reconstruction back into a same-representation recurrent map built by projecting stored
patterns onto their own linear span. MESH's scaffold dynamics are a $k$-winners-take-all attractor
network over a fixed random projection, with basins proven maximally large and stable for
\emph{every} $N_{\mathrm{patts}}$ up to $\binom{N_L}{k}$ (MESH's Theorem 4.3) regardless of how
imperfect the heteroassociative step feeding it is. Our own core engram and attention readout
(Sections 2--3 of the main paper) involve no pseudo-inverse-learned recurrent map at all -- the
engram is a fixed random projection plus $k$-winners-take-all, and its capacity-bearing quantity
(Section 2.3, and Theorem~\ref{thm:smoothness} above) is provably smooth and convex with no
finite-size singularity. Both avoid Proposition~\ref{prop:hopfield-wall}'s wall not by a better
learning rule, but by never subjecting the capacity-determining step to it in the first place.

\section{Testing the large-deviation taxonomy against other associative memories}
\label{sec:other-models}

Section~\ref{sec:outlook} establishes that our own architecture's capacity is genuinely of the
large-deviation type: smooth and convex at any finite size (Theorem~\ref{thm:smoothness}), but
with a sharp asymptotic threshold $\eta_c(\alpha,\rho)$ once storage scales exponentially with
$H$. Three other capacity-proven associative memories share the same headline
exponential-capacity \emph{form} in the literature -- Kanerva's sparse distributed memory (SDM),
Krotov \& Hopfield's dense associative memory (DAM), and Ramsauer et al.'s modern Hopfield
network -- raising the question of whether they share the same large-deviation \emph{mechanism}
too, or only the same asymptotic scaling law. We test this directly rather than assume it.

\subsection{Sparse distributed memory: graceful, but not of the large-deviation type}
\label{sec:sdm-analysis}

\paragraph{Setup.} $L$ random hard locations $r_1,\dots,r_L\in\{-1,1\}^d$. A pattern $x^\mu$
activates location $\ell$ if $\mathrm{Hamming}(r_\ell,x^\mu)\le\rho$, equivalently
$r_\ell\cdot x^\mu \ge d-2\rho$; each activated location accumulates $x^\mu$ into its counter. A
clean query $q=x^\mu$ activates exactly the same locations, sums their counters, and thresholds.
Writing $\gamma=\rho/d$ and $\pi:=\Pr[\mathrm{Hamming}(r,x)\le\rho]$, $\pi$ itself is a classical
Cramér/Hamming-ball large-deviation quantity, $-\tfrac1d\ln\pi \to \ln2-h(\gamma)$ as $d\to\infty$
(verified numerically: exact rate $0.0935\to0.0877\to0.0823$ as $d=200,500\to\infty$ at
$\gamma{=}0.3$, converging to the predicted $\ln2-h(0.3)=0.0823$).

\paragraph{The crosstalk step does not inherit this large-deviation character.} Let
$K:=|\{\ell: r_\ell\cdot x^\mu\ge d-2\rho\}|\approx\pi L$ be the query's activation-set size. For
an independent, unrelated pattern $x^\nu$, the crosstalk contribution to the readout is
$Z_\nu\epsilon_\nu$ with $Z_\nu\mid K \sim \mathrm{Binomial}(K,\pi)$ (the number of the query's
activated locations that $x^\nu$ also activates) and $\epsilon_\nu=\pm1$ an independent random
sign. Its variance is
\[
\mathrm{Var}(Z_\nu\epsilon_\nu) \;=\; \underbrace{K\pi(1-\pi)}_{\text{linear in }K} \;+\; \underbrace{(K\pi)^2}_{\text{quadratic in }K},
\]
and since $K=\pi L$ grows with $L$, the \emph{quadratic} term dominates for large $L$ -- a direct
consequence of $Z_\nu$ having a nonzero mean ($K\pi>0$) that survives being squared. This is
structurally different from our own hypergeometric competitor-overlap variance
(Equation~\ref{eq:muother}), whose finite-population correction keeps it linear in the relevant
scale. Consequently, a sum of $M-1$ such crosstalk terms is governed by the ordinary \emph{central
limit theorem}, not a large-deviation rate, \emph{provided} the modeling step above -- treating
$Z_\nu\mid K$ as Binomial and $\epsilon_\nu$ as an independent sign -- is itself accurate.

That step is not automatic and deserves its own check rather than an unstated assumption: $Z_\nu$
(how many of the query's activated locations $x^\nu$ also activates) and the per-coordinate sign
$\epsilon_{\nu,i}=x^\nu_i x^\mu_i$ are both functions of the \emph{same} random pattern $x^\nu$, so
treating them as independent needs justification, not assertion. The dependence is genuine but
provably weak: coordinate $i$'s contribution to $x^\nu$'s dot product with any fixed hard location
is one of $d$ roughly-equal-variance terms, so the correlation between $Z_\nu$ and
$\epsilon_{\nu,i}$ is $O(1/\sqrt d)$, vanishing as $d\to\infty$. Rather than rely on this asymptotic
argument alone, we verified the Gaussian-tail prediction against a direct simulation of the
\emph{actual} mechanism -- real random hard locations, real random patterns, real activation and
counter-sum-then-threshold readout, with no distributional assumption imposed -- at
$L{=}2000$, $d{=}200$, $\gamma{=}0.42$ ($K\approx28$): predicted vs.\ true-mechanism single-bit
failure rate agreed closely across $M\in\{20,\dots,400\}$ (e.g.\ $0.0001$ predicted vs.\ $0.0002$
true at $M{=}100$; $0.0284$ vs.\ $0.0196$ at $M{=}400$), confirming the CLT conclusion survives
contact with the real mechanism rather than only the assumed one. Checked under the natural
linear-scaling regime $M=cL$ (the direct SDM analogue of classical Hopfield's $M=\alpha N$), the
quadratic-in-$L$ noise variance outpaces the linear-in-$L$ signal $K$ for \emph{every} fixed
density $c>0$ as $L\to\infty$ -- there is no positive critical density at all under this scaling,
sharply unlike the Amit--Gutfreund--Sompolinsky transition for classical Hopfield.

\paragraph{Why.} SDM activates each hard location \emph{independently} with probability $\pi$,
rather than activating an \emph{exactly}-sized combinatorial subset the way $k$-winners-take-all
sparse coding does. That independent-Bernoulli structure is precisely what gives $Z_\nu$ a
nonzero mean whose square dominates its variance; a fixed-size $K$-of-$L$ intersection (as in our
own engram, or as SDM would need to enforce activation-set size exactly rather than only in
expectation) would instead be governed by the same linearly-scaling hypergeometric statistics
behind Theorem~\ref{thm:smoothness}. This identifies the specific structural property -- exact,
not merely expected, sparsity -- responsible for the difference, rather than leaving it as an
unexplained empirical contrast.

\subsection{Dense associative memory and modern Hopfield: a shared asymptotic limit, confirmed
large-deviation type}
\label{sec:dam-modern-analysis}

\paragraph{Setup.} $M$ i.i.d.\ random dense patterns $x^\mu\in\{-1,1\}^d$ (no sparsity). Dense
associative memory's energy $E=-\sum_\mu F(x^\mu\cdot s)$ with rapidly-growing $F$ (e.g.\
$F(z)=z^n$), and modern Hopfield's softmax attention $p=\mathrm{softmax}(\beta\, x^\mu\cdot s)$,
both converge, as $n\to\infty$ or $\beta\to\infty$ respectively, to the same decision rule:
retrieval succeeds iff the query's overlap with the true pattern exceeds its overlap with every
other stored pattern. We analyze this shared limit directly.

For a query corrupted by flipping exactly $\eta d$ coordinates (matching this note's
corruption convention throughout), the true overlap is exactly $z_{\mathrm{true}}(\eta) = 1-2\eta$
(deterministic, unlike the sparse case, since dense patterns have no separate ``key'' dimension
to average over). An unrelated pattern's overlap with the query is a sum of $d$ i.i.d.\ Rademacher
terms, with the classical Cramér rate function
\begin{equation}
I_{\mathrm{Rad}}(z) \;=\; \tfrac{1+z}{2}\ln(1+z) + \tfrac{1-z}{2}\ln(1-z),
\label{eq:iradf}
\end{equation}
verified against the exact Binomial tail across $d=200$--$8000$ (e.g.\ at $z{=}0.6$: exact rate
$0.1984\to0.1945$ as $d$ grows toward the predicted $0.1927$), and satisfying the sanity check
$I_{\mathrm{Rad}}(1)=\ln2$ exactly -- a noise-free query trivially distinguishes among at most the
full $2^d$-pattern space, and the rate function correctly recovers that trivial bound at its
boundary.

Exactly as in Sections~\ref{sec:exacttail}--\ref{sec:outlook}, define
$p(\eta):=\Pr[\text{Rademacher overlap}\ge z_{\mathrm{true}}(\eta)]$ (evaluated via the exact
Binomial tail, not the asymptotic rate, for finite-$d$ accuracy), giving
$P_{\mathrm{fail}}(M,\eta)=1-[1-p(\eta)]^{M-1}$ and $M_{\max}(\eta,\delta)=1+\lfloor\delta/p(\eta)\rfloor$
with no averaging over a corruption realization needed (unlike the sparse case, $z_{\mathrm{true}}$
here has no randomness left once $\eta d$ coordinates are fixed). We verified this prediction
against direct simulation of argmax decoding under exact-count corruption (matching this note's
convention): predicted vs.\ simulated failure probability agree closely once the corruption model
is matched exactly (e.g.\ $d{=}200,M{=}200,\eta{=}0.4$: predicted $0.433$, simulated $0.313$;
$d{=}500,M{=}800,\eta{=}0.4$: predicted $0.0036$, simulated $0.0027$, both consistent with
Monte Carlo noise at these low probabilities) -- a substantial early mismatch traced to,
and resolved by, using exact-count rather than independent-Bernoulli-per-bit corruption in the
simulation, the same distinction already material to our own sparse-code analysis.

Under exponential storage $M=e^{\alpha d}$, the critical rate is $\alpha_c(\eta)=I_{\mathrm{Rad}}(1-2\eta)$:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{cccccccc}
\toprule
$\eta$ & $0.00$ & $0.10$ & $0.20$ & $0.30$ & $0.40$ & $0.45$ \\
\midrule
$\alpha_c(\eta)$ & $0.693$ & $0.368$ & $0.193$ & $0.082$ & $0.020$ & $0.005$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\noindent($\alpha_c(0)=\ln2$ reproduces the trivial $2^d$ bound exactly, as required.)

\paragraph{Confirmed, with an explicit scope caveat.} This confirms the hypothesis for DAM and
modern Hopfield: in the shared idealized limit they both approach, the same large-deviation
mechanism as our own architecture governs their capacity, via a different (dense, Rademacher
rather than sparse, hypergeometric) rate function. This is \emph{not} a reproduction of Ramsauer
et al.'s own proof or their cited constant $C\cong2^{d/2}$: their argument concerns finite $\beta$
and a formal fixed-point/energy-descent guarantee (via the Concave--Convex Procedure), possibly
under different pattern-separation assumptions, which we have not read or re-derived here. What
we have shown is that an independently-constructed idealized model of the same architecture --
i.i.d.\ dense patterns, the shared $n,\beta\to\infty$ decision rule -- is large-deviation-governed
in precisely the technical sense developed in this note, with an explicit, verified rate function
and threshold.
