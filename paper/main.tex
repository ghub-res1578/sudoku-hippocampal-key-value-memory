\begin{abstract}
We study a hippocampal-inspired memory architecture in which solved Sudoku grids are stored
as sparse binary engrams, formed by projecting each grid's $81\times81$ same-digit
coincidence matrix through a fixed random sparse matrix and taking the top-$K$ active units.
An attention-based key-value readout retrieves stored digit values with no observed capacity
ceiling up to $M=2000$ stored items and tolerance to $30\%$ query corruption, in sharp
contrast to a ridge-regression readout, which collapses to chance performance at $M{\sim}H$
even without any query noise. Retrieval degrades gracefully, not catastrophically, under
heavier query corruption too: accuracy holds to $35\%$ of engram bits flipped and then
declines over a narrow window whose rescue by conflict-mask cleanup peaks sharply at $42\%$
noise. Partial-grid cues drive an iterative attractor that recovers missing digits with
graceful, clue-count-dependent accuracy. We then ask whether the
symbolic legality check that this architecture relies on for error correction --
Sudoku's row/column/box constraint -- can instead emerge from neural dynamics rather than
being hard-coded. We build a leaky-integrate-and-fire spiking network whose only structural
knowledge of Sudoku is which cell-pairs are \emph{forbidden} from sharing a digit
(realized as inhibitory coupling) and which are not (realized as weak excitatory coupling).
Driven by per-cell currents encoding candidate digit identities, the network's steady-state
spike timing reproduces the coincidence matrix without any digit ever being decoded from a
single neuron's activity: same-digit cells synchronize to within $0.2$\,ms while
differently-digited cells separate by ${\sim}13$\,ms, consistently across eight independently
sampled grids. Reading out this emergent, synchrony-based coincidence matrix and feeding it
through the \emph{same} associative engram pipeline used for storage yields completion
accuracy that is statistically indistinguishable (Wilcoxon signed-rank, $p>0.05$ at every
tested clue fraction, paired $n=20$) from the original hard-coded conflict mask, while
substantially exceeding the accuracy of the raw single-shot associative guess it starts
from. This dynamical mechanism is itself robust to noise: corrupting known cells' drive
currents with Gaussian jitter degrades completion accuracy smoothly, remaining above the
noise-free associative baseline even at current noise more than twice the spacing between
adjacent digit levels. We also characterize a reproducible power-law crossover in attractor
relaxation time
near the completion threshold, and report -- rather than paper over -- that its exponent is
not universal across stored-set sizes, tempering any claim to a shared critical-phenomena
universality class. Finally, we extend the engram layer to a second modality -- natural
images, heteroassociated to Sudoku engrams via the same bidirectional pseudo-inverse rule
used by Vector-HaSH \cite{chandra2025} -- and benchmark it against classical Hopfield networks.
The comparison is double-edged: a paired nearest-neighbor baseline beats our pipeline at every
tested stored-set size, and the gap widens rather than closes as that size grows ($+3.5$ to
$+96.0$ percentage points from $M{=}200$ to $M{=}2000$), an honest limitation we report rather
than omit; but on the specific property Vector-HaSH is designed to provide, our architecture
succeeds where classical and even pseudo-inverse-trained Hopfield networks -- the latter using
the identical learning rule we do, and statistically tied with ours in noise tolerance at fixed
storage size -- collapse to zero recovery an order of magnitude sooner in storage capacity,
isolating the separate error-correcting attractor, not the learning rule, as the source of the
graceful degradation. This capacity-scaling advantage does not, however, extend to the broader
associative-memory literature: benchmarked further against Modern Hopfield networks and dense
associative memory, given properly re-validated (not naively carried-over) separation
sharpness, both substantially outperform our raw recovery accuracy at high storage load under
matched noise ($99.0\%$ and $96.0\%$ vs.\ our $57.5\%$ at $M{=}800$), a gap a $3\times$ larger
engram narrows by about half but does not close -- an honest limitation we report because the
architecture's case rests elsewhere: unlike every alternative tested, whose stored
representation \emph{is} the content itself, our key (a sparse engram drawn from an
astronomically large combinatorial space) is entirely decoupled from the value it is bound to,
letting one fixed key infrastructure serve arbitrary content \emph{and} the original
Sudoku-legality task at once, while still matching these purpose-built alternatives at
low-to-moderate noise. A separation-operator ablation -- swapping the readout's softmax
nonlinearity for five alternatives spanning the correlation-matrix-memory, sparse-distributed-memory,
and dense-associative-memory literatures -- shows that noise robustness tracks whether an
operator sharpens by an absolute similarity gap or a similarity ratio, not merely how
aggressively it discriminates: ratio-based sharpening collapses at roughly half the noise level
gap-based sharpening tolerates, given matched zero-noise selectivity. We also verify directly,
rather than assume by analogy to sparse random-projection hashing circuits, that the encoder
preserves relative similarity between inputs (Spearman $\rho=0.97$ between raw coincidence-matrix
similarity and engram overlap across the full corruption range). We additionally build Vector-HaSH's
actual modular addressing mechanism, not just its heteroassociation rule, and find it beats our
own flat scaffold once given a genuinely recurrent (not independent) cross-module correction step
-- a result against our own architecture, obtained by testing rather than only citing a
comparison. A complexity analysis clarifies what this buys: neither scaffold escapes $O(M)$
query-time growth, the same class occupied by $k$-nearest-neighbor and the sharpest associative
memories tested; the genuine advantage is a discrimination capacity decoupled from content
dimensionality and an $O(M)$-index storage cost, while content fidelity remains bounded by the
same image effective-rank ceiling for every method. All code, data, and reproduction
instructions accompany this paper.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Biological memory systems, and the hippocampus in particular, are widely understood to store
information as sparse, combinatorial codes and to retrieve it through content-addressable,
key-value-like association rather than by address \cite{marr1971,rolls2013,hopfield1982}. This
division of labor is formalized by the complementary learning systems framework, which casts
the hippocampus and neocortex as a fast, sparse, pattern-separated store and a slow,
overlapping, generalizing one, respectively \cite{mcclelland1995}, and has recently been
argued to be the same key-value factorization -- discriminable keys, content-faithful values
-- that underlies the success of attention-based machine-learning systems
\cite{gershman2025}.
A recurring computational question for such architectures is how they resolve
\emph{ambiguity}: when a retrieval cue is noisy, partial, or internally inconsistent, some
mechanism must clean it up before or during recall. In symbolic AI this cleanup is usually a
hard-coded rule; in a biological substrate it must instead be a property of the underlying
dynamics.

We use Sudoku as a controlled testbed for this question. A solved Sudoku grid is a natural
combinatorial object: 81 cells, each carrying one of nine digits, subject to the constraint
that no two cells sharing a row, column, or $3\times3$ box carry the same digit. This gives a
well-defined, tunable notion of query difficulty (the fraction of cells given as clues) and an
unambiguous ground truth against which any completion can be checked.

We build our architecture in three layers, corresponding to three literatures we intend this
work to speak to:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Combinatorial coding.} Each stored grid's relational structure (which
  cell-pairs share a digit) is projected through a fixed random sparse matrix and sparsified
  to a $k$-winner-take-all binary engram -- a scheme in the spirit of Willshaw associative
  memory \cite{willshaw1969} and sparse random-projection coding, closely related to the
  random-projection-plus-winner-take-all hashing circuit identified in the fly olfactory
  system \cite{dasgupta2017}. We test directly, in Section~\ref{sec:hashing}, whether our
  encoder shares that circuit's defining property: approximate preservation of relative
  similarity between inputs.
  \item \textbf{Key-value memory.} Retrieval uses an attention-style readout -- softmax
  similarity between a query engram and all stored engrams, weighting the corresponding
  stored values -- rather than a single learned linear map, echoing the connection between
  classical correlation-matrix memory \cite{kohonen1972}, sparse distributed memory
  \cite{kanerva1988}, classical and modern dense associative memory
  \cite{krotov2016,ramsauer2020}, and Transformer-style self-attention
  \cite{vaswani2017,schlag2021} -- four architectures recently unified as instances of the
  same key-value memory differing only in the nonlinearity (``separation operator'') applied
  to key-query similarity \cite{gershman2025}. Section~\ref{sec:separation} tests that
  unification directly, swapping our readout's separation operator across all four families.
  \item \textbf{Constraint satisfaction as dynamics.} We ask whether Sudoku's legality
  constraint, which the architecture above uses explicitly as a hard-coded cleanup rule, can
  instead be realized as an emergent property of a spiking network's synchronization
  dynamics -- reading out relational structure from spike-timing coincidence in the spirit of
  the classical correlation theory of binding-by-synchrony \cite{malsburg1981,singer1995}, and
  using relaxation to a coupled-oscillator fixed point to enforce a combinatorial constraint in
  the spirit of Hopfield and Tank's neural optimization networks \cite{hopfieldtank1985}. This
  is also a question in the spirit of the broader statistical mechanics of constraint
  satisfaction and random $k$-SAT \cite{mezard2002,mezard2009}, which we invoke here as
  motivating context rather than as a universality result we claim to extend. Unlike gradient
  descent on an energy function or symbolic backtracking search -- the two standard ways to
  settle a combinatorial constraint problem, and the mechanisms every associative-memory
  baseline we compare against in Section~\ref{sec:hetero} ultimately relies on -- this
  mechanism has no encoded objective function at all: local excitatory/inhibitory coupling and
  emergent phase synchrony do the work.
\end{itemize}

We additionally ask, in Section~\ref{sec:hetero}, whether the engram layer generalizes beyond
a single content type. Every classical associative memory couples its storage capacity to its
own neuron count: a Hopfield-style network's stored patterns \emph{are} its state vector, so
capacity is inescapably a function of that vector's dimension. We instead heteroassociate
natural images to Sudoku engrams using the bidirectional pseudo-inverse mechanism of
Vector-HaSH \cite{chandra2025}, so that the \emph{key} -- a sparse engram drawn from an
astronomically large combinatorial space, valid $9\times9$ grids numbering on the order of
$6.7\times10^{21}$ -- is entirely decoupled from the \emph{value} it is bound to, which can be
arbitrary content of any dimension. We benchmark the result directly against classical Hopfield
networks, and, further, against Modern Hopfield networks, sparse distributed memory, and dense
associative memory (Section~\ref{sec:modern-comparison}) -- three architectures whose stored
representation \emph{is} the content itself -- to test directly what this decoupling costs and
what it buys.

Our central empirical result is that these three layers compose: a physically-implemented,
dynamics-based legality check, built entirely from local excitatory/inhibitory coupling with
no reference to Sudoku's symbolic rules beyond \emph{which cells must differ}, is
statistically indistinguishable from the hard-coded rule it replaces, once routed through the
same combinatorial-coding and key-value-recall machinery. We view the value of this result as
existence-proof and mechanism, not as an improvement over a rule that was never
computationally difficult to hard-code in the first place.

\section{Model}

\subsection{Coincidence-matrix engram and key-value readout}

A solved grid $g$ is an assignment of digits $\{1,\dots,9\}$ to $N=81$ cells. Its
\emph{coincidence matrix} $C\in\{0,1\}^{N\times N}$ records which cell pairs share a digit:
$C_{ij} = \mathbb{1}[g_i = g_j]$ for $i\neq j$. This matrix, flattened, is the ``key source''
for a stored memory.

A fixed random sparse matrix $W_{SH}\in\mathbb{R}^{H\times N^2}$ (density $\rho$, nonzero
entries drawn i.i.d.\ from $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$) projects the flattened coincidence matrix into
an $H$-dimensional hippocampal layer. The engram is the top-$K$ $k$-winner-take-all
binarization of this projection:
\begin{equation}
h = \mathrm{topK}\!\left(W_{SH}\,\mathrm{vec}(C)\right) \in \{0,1\}^H .
\end{equation}
Each stored grid also has an associated \emph{value} vector $b\in\mathbb{R}^N$, a per-cell
digit-to-drive-level mapping.

Given $M$ stored (key, value) pairs $\{(h_m, b_m)\}_{m=1}^M$ and a query engram $h_q$, the
\textbf{attention readout} is
\begin{equation}
\hat b = \sum_{m=1}^{M} p_m\, b_m, \qquad
p = \mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\frac{H_{\mathrm{keys}}\, h_q}{\tau}\right),
\end{equation}
a pure content-addressable lookup with no learned parameters beyond the fixed encoder. We
compare this against a \textbf{ridge-regression readout}, which instead learns a single
linear map $W_{HD}$ (via the standard ridge solution) such that $W_{HD} h_m \approx b_m$ for
all stored pairs, and retrieves via $\hat b = W_{HD} h_q$.

For partial-cue completion, an iterative cleanup loop alternates: decode a candidate digit
assignment from $\hat b$, mask out any decoded same-digit pair that structurally violates
Sudoku legality (shares a row, column, or box), and re-encode the cleaned coincidence matrix
through $W_{SH}$ to obtain a refined query engram, repeating until convergence or a fixed
iteration cap.

\subsection{Spiking network}

The spiking network consists of $N=81$ excitatory leaky-integrate-and-fire (LIF) neurons,
one per cell, each paired with a fast inhibitory relay neuron. Membrane potential obeys
\begin{equation}
\tau_m \,\dot v = I_d + I_{\mathrm{osc}}(t) - v, \qquad
I_{\mathrm{osc}}(t) = 0.5 - 0.2\cos(2\pi f_{\mathrm{osc}} t),
\end{equation}
with $\tau_m = 40$\,ms, $f_{\mathrm{osc}} = 10$\,Hz (a shared oscillatory drive, common to
every neuron), threshold $V_{\mathrm{th}}=1$, and reset to $0$. $I_d$ is a per-neuron DC
current encoding a candidate digit identity: nine equally spaced levels in a range chosen so
that every digit reliably crosses threshold once per oscillation cycle
(Section~\ref{sec:sync}).

The network's \emph{only} structural knowledge of Sudoku is the conflict mask
$\mathrm{Conf}_{ij}$, equal to $1$ if cells $i,j$ share a row, column, or box and $0$
otherwise, and its complement. Conflicting cell pairs are coupled through the inhibitory
relay (a fixed weight $w_{IE}$);
non-conflicting pairs are coupled through direct weak excitatory synapses (weight $w_{EE}$).
No synapse encodes which cells \emph{should} share a digit -- that information is never
given structurally, only through the per-cell drive current, exactly mirroring the role of a
query in the key-value architecture above. Reading a relation (``these cells share a digit'')
out of spike-timing coincidence rather than out of any single neuron's firing rate is a direct
instance of the correlation theory of binding-by-synchrony
\cite{malsburg1981,singer1995}: Sections~\ref{sec:sync}--\ref{sec:emergent} test whether that
hypothesis, applied to this specific combinatorial relation, actually works.

\subsection{The engram code as a similarity-preserving hash}
\label{sec:hashing}

Every noise-robustness result in this paper (Table~\ref{tab:collapse} above, and
Sections~\ref{sec:graceful-noise}, \ref{sec:emergent}, \ref{sec:drive-noise} below) measures
\emph{downstream decode accuracy} degrading gracefully as the query is corrupted. None of them
directly tests the encoder's own geometry: whether $\mathrm{topK}(W_{SH}\,\mathrm{vec}(C))$
approximately preserves relative similarity between inputs, the property that random
projection followed by winner-take-all sparsification is known to provide in the fly olfactory
mushroom body \cite{dasgupta2017}. We test this directly rather than assume it by analogy.

For 30 randomly sampled stored grids, we corrupted each grid's true coincidence matrix
$C_{\mathrm{true}}$ by flipping a controlled fraction of its (symmetric) same-digit entries,
swept from $0\%$ to $100\%$, and measured two quantities on each corrupted instance: the
\emph{input similarity} to the true grid (Jaccard overlap of the same-digit pair sets between
$C_{\mathrm{noisy}}$ and $C_{\mathrm{true}}$) and the \emph{engram similarity} of the resulting
codes ($|h_{\mathrm{noisy}}\cap h_{\mathrm{true}}|/K$). Across $390$ corrupted instances
spanning the full corruption range, the two are strongly and monotonically related (Spearman
$\rho=0.974$, $p<10^{-250}$; Figure~\ref{fig:hashing}) -- direct confirmation that the encoder
behaves as a similarity-preserving hash rather than merely as a randomizing sparsifier. The
floor this relationship approaches at full corruption ($0.149$ mean engram overlap at $0\%$
input similarity) matches the chance overlap expected between two independent $K$-sparse codes
in an $H$-dimensional space, $K/H=150/1000=0.15$, almost exactly -- confirming that residual
overlap at maximal corruption reflects code geometry, not a leftover correlation the corruption
procedure failed to remove.

\subsection{Separation operators: gap-based vs.\ ratio-based sharpening}
\label{sec:separation}

The attention readout above uses softmax as its separation operator. Gershman, Fiete \& Irie
\cite{gershman2025} note that softmax, a hard top-$j$ threshold, and a rectified-power
nonlinearity are, respectively, the separation operators of Transformer-style attention,
sparse distributed memory \cite{kanerva1988}, and dense associative memory
\cite{krotov2016,ramsauer2020} -- three architectures differing only in this one nonlinearity,
alongside plain (identity) correlation-matrix memory \cite{kohonen1972}, which applies no
separation at all. We test this unification directly: holding the encoder, the stored $M{=}200$
engrams, and every corrupted query identical (a fully paired design), we swap only the
separation operator and re-run the query-noise sweep of Section~\ref{sec:graceful-noise},
without the conflict-mask cleanup pass, to isolate the operator's own behavior.

\begin{center}
\includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{figures/separation_operator_ablation.png}
\end{center}
\refstepcounter{figure}\label{fig:separation}
\noindent\textbf{Figure \thefigure.} Digit-cell retrieval accuracy vs.\ query noise for six separation operators,
evaluated on identical corrupted queries at every noise level ($M{=}200$, $n{=}100$/point, no
cleanup pass).

Two findings depart from the intuition that a separation operator's robustness is set by how
aggressively it discriminates (``sharp separation is powerful but fragile''
\cite{gershman2025}). First, identity separation -- no nonlinearity at all -- performs at
chance \emph{even with a perfectly clean query} ($11.4\%$ vs.\ $100\%$ for softmax at $0\%$
noise): with $M{=}200$ competing memories, a linear reweighting cannot overcome the combined
pull of the other $199$ stored values on the weighted average, so some separating nonlinearity
is not merely a robustness refinement here but a strict requirement for retrieval to function
at all. Second, and more surprisingly, sharper is not uniformly more fragile: a hard-threshold
top-$5$ average (naively implementing sparse distributed memory's equal-weight readout) is
\emph{also} near chance at every noise level, including zero, because averaging five
equally-weighted stored values -- one correct, four spurious -- dilutes the correct digit
rather than recovering it, unlike real sparse distributed memory's per-bit majority vote over
redundant patterns. Meanwhile argmax (winner-take-all, the ``ideal noiseless'' operator
\cite{gershman2025}) and a sharpened softmax ($\tau=0.5$) hold up \emph{at least as well as}
the baseline softmax ($\tau=2.0$) right up to a shared collapse near $45$--$48\%$ noise --
sharpness itself is not the fault line.

The real fault line is gap-based vs.\ ratio-based sharpening. The rectified-polynomial
operator (dense-associative-memory-style, exponent $n=8$) collapses far earlier, around
$20$--$25\%$ noise, despite normalized zero-noise selectivity comparable to the other sharp
operators. We traced this mechanistically: even at zero noise, the best-competing wrong memory
among the $M{=}200$ stored grids already reaches, on average, similarity within a factor of
$3.39$ of the true match's (an extreme-value effect of having $199$ competitors, well below the
naive factor of $6.67$ predicted from the mean chance-overlap floor alone), and this ratio
shrinks toward $1$ as noise grows ($1.92$ at $20\%$ noise, $1.45$ at $30\%$). A power of a
ratio shrinking toward $1$ loses discriminative weight far faster than an exponential of the
corresponding absolute gap does, for the same starting margin -- softmax's exponential-of-a-gap
form is intrinsically more robust than a power-of-a-ratio form once a sizable number of
competing memories guarantees a non-negligible interference floor. This is a refinement, not a
contradiction, of the unification in \cite{gershman2025}: the four architectures are indeed the
same computation with a different nonlinearity plugged in, but which nonlinearity is the right
engineering choice depends on the similarity statistics of the memory system it operates over,
not on sharpness alone.

\subsection{Relaxation dynamics near the completion threshold}
\label{sec:crossover}

The iteration count of the attractor loop, as a function of clue fraction, shows a
reproducible dynamical signature reminiscent of relaxation-time crossovers studied in the
statistical mechanics of random constraint satisfaction \cite{mezard2002}. This is the same
basic move as Hopfield and Tank's classical use of attractor relaxation to solve constrained
optimization problems \cite{hopfieldtank1985}, applied here to a combinatorial completion task
with a well-defined difficulty axis (clue fraction) rather than a single fixed instance. For
the sharpest
transition we found ($M=500$, $K=400$), a sub-critical plateau ($92.6\pm7.1$ iterations,
clue fraction $\leq 0.21$) decays as a power law,
$\tau \sim (p_c - p)^{\nu}$, to a floor of exactly 2 iterations by clue fraction $0.53$.
Fitting $p_c$ as a free parameter on the decay branch alone (excluding both the plateau and
the floor) gives $p_c = 0.395$ -- matching the independently observed floor onset almost
exactly -- and $\nu = 1.32\pm0.08$ ($R^2=0.978$; Figure~\ref{fig:crossover}).

\end{center}

We emphasize an honest limitation here. Repeating this fit for four additional $(M,K)$
configurations gives exponents ranging from $\nu=0.47$ to $\nu=3.75$ -- an eightfold spread
too large to attribute to sampling noise alone. We do not have a validated universal
exponent, and we report this rather than omit it: the crossover phenomenon itself replicates
robustly (a clean power-law fit, $R^2\geq0.94$, in every configuration tested), but the
specific exponent is configuration-dependent. We therefore treat Section~\ref{sec:crossover}
as a secondary, honestly-scoped characterization of the architecture's dynamics, not as a
claimed critical-phenomena universality result.

\subsection{Spiking network: phase synchrony as an emergent conflict mask}
\label{sec:sync}

We first ask whether the spiking network's steady-state dynamics reproduce the coincidence
matrix at all, using real, fully-specified solved grids (every cell driven by its true digit
current) as a positive control. Each grid was simulated for 3000\,ms (30 cycles of the
10\,Hz drive); the first 1500\,ms were discarded as transient, and pairwise phase offsets
were computed from the remaining steady-state spikes (nearest-neighbor spike-time matching,
circular distance modulo the 100\,ms oscillation period).

At the operating point used throughout ($w_{IE}=0.005$, $w_{EE}=0.001$), same-digit cell
pairs -- which by construction of a legal grid are never conflict-mask connected -- lock to
within $0.205\pm0.023$\,ms of each other, while differently-digited pairs separate by
${\sim}13$\,ms, regardless of whether they happen to be inhibition-connected
($12.97\pm0.18$\,ms) or excitation-connected ($13.00\pm0.12$\,ms). This pattern is
essentially identical across eight independently sampled grids (Table~\ref{tab:multigrid}),
and visibly forms a clean, reproducible ``phase comb'' each oscillation cycle
(Figure~\ref{fig:raster}).

Because a legal grid never puts the same digit on two conflicting cells, the numbers above
conflate the current-driven separation between different digits with any additional,
connection-type-specific effect of inhibition. To isolate the latter, we assigned each cell
a \emph{random} digit label (ignoring legality) and measured the phase offset specifically
between same-driven pairs, split by connection type. At the validated operating point, the
inhibition-connected same-driven pairs show $2.0$\,ms more desynchronization than
excitation-connected same-driven pairs, above a $\sim1$\,ms baseline gap present even with
all coupling switched off (a structural artifact of the two pair categories, not a synaptic
effect) -- a real, if modest, causal contribution of the inhibitory wiring. This effect
requires a specific weight balance: because each cell has roughly three times as many
excitatory (complement-mask) partners as inhibitory (conflict-mask) partners, equal or
near-equal per-synapse weights let the aggregate excitatory pull dominate and synchronize the
\emph{entire} population regardless of connection type, erasing the effect above
$w_{EE}\gtrsim0.003$.

We also explicitly tested, and ruled out, a stronger hypothesis: that conflicting pairs
might settle into a genuine anti-phase lock (half-cycle separation), the mechanism underlying
half-center oscillators in central pattern generators. Scanning inhibitory strength across an
80-fold range ($w_{IE}=0.005$ to $0.4$) produced no discernible trend toward anti-phase
locking (steady-state offset flat at $3.4$--$3.6$\,ms throughout). This is a structural
consequence of the shared oscillatory drive: because every neuron receives an \emph{identical}
periodic forcing term, each oscillation cycle provides a fresh, drive-dominated
threshold-crossing opportunity that a locally-scaled inhibitory kick cannot postpone past,
regardless of its strength. Genuine anti-phase encoding would require either removing this
common pacemaker or making inhibition strong enough to suppress firing for an entire cycle,
neither of which holds in the present architecture.

Finally, we asked whether a fixed, small coincidence window could serve as a sharp
same-digit/different-digit classifier on its own, independent of any downstream cleanup. Over
a $0.5$--$10$\,ms threshold scan (four connectivity configurations, $n=600$ pairs each), the
best achievable balanced accuracy was $63$--$65\%$: real but soft, with substantial overlap
between the two distributions (excitation-connected pairs' 90th percentile offset,
${\sim}5$\,ms, exceeds inhibition-connected pairs' 10th percentile, ${\sim}1.3$\,ms). A
single pairwise check is not, by itself, a reliable decision rule.

\subsection{Emergent coincidence matrix closes the loop}
\label{sec:emergent}

We now close the loop: rather than decoding a digit from any single neuron's activity, we
build an emergent coincidence matrix $\hat C$ directly from the network's steady-state
pairwise synchrony, thresholding each pair's phase offset at $\theta=2$\,ms (the gap
identified in Section~\ref{sec:sync}). $\hat C$ is fed through the \emph{same} $W_{SH}$
projection, $k$-winner-take-all, and attention-readout pipeline used for storage and
retrieval throughout this paper -- the spiking network's only role is to supply the
relational input that the hard-coded conflict mask previously supplied.

\subsection{Graceful degradation under drive-current noise}
\label{sec:drive-noise}

Two different kinds of robustness have been tested so far: corruption of the abstract
engram vector itself (Section~\ref{sec:graceful-noise}, random bit-flips) and missing
per-cell information (Section~\ref{sec:emergent}, unknown cells driven by the associative
engram's own guess). Here we test a third, specific to the spiking mechanism: corruption of
the physical drive current itself. Rather than any cell being entirely unknown, every
\emph{known} cell's drive current was perturbed with Gaussian jitter before driving the
network (clue fraction fixed at $0.30$; unknown cells still use the associative engram's own
guess, unperturbed; $n=20$ trials/point, paired across noise levels). Figure~\ref{fig:drive-noise}
shows the result: accuracy declines smoothly as
current noise grows from $0$ to over twice the spacing between adjacent digit levels, with no
sign of a cliff. Notably, even at the highest tested noise ($\sigma$ equal to $2.2\times$ the
inter-digit spacing -- comparable to smearing a query halfway to its neighboring digit level
on average) the dynamics pipeline ($58.1\%$) still exceeds the noise-free symbolic-guess
baseline it is compared against ($51.9\%$): the mechanism does not merely tolerate drive
noise gracefully, it remains a net improvement over the associative engram's own single-shot
guess even when its input is substantially corrupted.

\subsection{Why low clue fractions are hard: signal quality, not signal quantity}

A natural hypothesis for the low-clue-fraction bottleneck is that too few cells are
confidently driven to produce enough coincident activity to strongly activate the correct
stored engram. We tested this directly and found the opposite: at clue fraction $0.19$, the
emergent $\hat C$ has \emph{more} active (same-digit-labeled) pairs on average ($527$) than
the true grid ($324$) -- the network is not under-active. The excess is almost entirely false
positives (consistent with the specificity numbers in Table~\ref{tab:emergent}), and these
spurious coincidences dilute the resulting engram's ability to discriminate the correct
stored memory among the $M$ candidates: the softmax attention readout places only $37.6\%$
of its mass on the true target at clue fraction $0.19$ (with, on average, ${\sim}52$ other
stored grids scoring higher similarity), rising to $65.5\%$ mass and a mean rank of $1.9$ at
clue fraction $0.30$. The mechanism limiting low-clue-fraction performance is therefore noise
in the query signal's specificity, not a shortage of active units -- a distinction that
points at specificity (not raw signal strength) as the lever for improving this regime.

\section{Extension: Cross-Modal Heteroassociation}
\label{sec:hetero}

Everything above stores and retrieves a single content type (Sudoku grids). Vector-HaSH
\cite{chandra2025}, and its non-spatial, modular-attractor sibling MESH \cite{sharma2022},
factor biological memory into two parts: a fixed, content-independent
\emph{scaffold} (grid-cell/hippocampal recurrence in Vector-HaSH, a modular fixed attractor
network in MESH) that provably generates exponentially many
attractors with equally large basins, and a separate, plastic \emph{heteroassociative} pathway
linking arbitrary sensory input to that scaffold via ``a Hebb-like online implementation of the
pseudoinverse rule.'' We test whether our architecture's engram layer can play the scaffold's
role for a genuinely different modality -- natural images -- using exactly that heteroassociative
mechanism, and we benchmark the result against classical Hopfield networks, whose well-known
memory cliff Vector-HaSH is explicitly designed to avoid.

\subsection{Architecture}
\label{sec:hetero-arch}

An image $x\in\mathbb{R}^{D}$ is linked to its paired grid's engram $h\in\{0,1\}^H$ by two
ridge-regularized pseudo-inverse matrices, $W_{SI}$ (image$\to$engram) and $W_{IS}$
(engram$\to$image), trained as a \emph{bidirectional, one-memory-per-pair} association, each
of the $M$ stored images given its own unique target engram -- not many exemplars regressed
onto one shared class target. We originally reported that alternative as collapsing sharply
under noise ($0.835\to0.330$ between $\sigma=0$ and $\sigma=0.10$); that test used the same
too-small default ridge diagnosed and corrected in Section~\ref{sec:capacity-hetero}, and we
have since verified, changing only the ridge value and nothing else, that it does not actually
cliff: properly regularized, the identical setup degrades gracefully ($0.846\to0.725$ over the
same step) and supports genuine, if coarse, classification ($78.8\%$ accuracy, exhaustive over
all $900$ held-out test images, decoding which of $9$ pooled classes a held-out image belongs
to at zero noise, declining gracefully with noise; an earlier $100$-trial sample overestimated
this at $86\%$). Section~\ref{sec:digit-classifier-confusion} examines this classifier's error
structure and its robustness to the random seed in detail. We correct the earlier claim rather
than let a regularization artifact stand as
evidence against shared targets in general. The real, structural reason one-memory-per-pair is
required for \emph{this} task is different: a shared target supports at most as many
distinguishable outputs as there are distinct targets (nine, in the corrected test above),
whereas recovering a \emph{specific} one of $M$ stored images requires $M$ mutually
distinguishable targets -- exactly what one-memory-per-pair provides and a shared per-class
target structurally cannot, regardless of regularization. Recall is a four-stage loop: a noisy image cue is
projected through $W_{SI}$ and thresholded to an initial engram; the existing attention readout
and conflict-mask cleanup (Section~2.1) iterate this engram to a Sudoku-consistent fixed point,
exactly as for a partial-grid cue; the cleaned engram is projected back through $W_{IS}$ to
reconstruct the image. Figure~\ref{fig:best-examples} shows representative cases where this
cleanup step turns a badly corrupted initial reconstruction into an exact one.

\subsection{A trivial baseline this architecture does not yet beat}
\label{sec:knn-baseline}

At $M{=}200$, a reviewer's natural objection is that this may reduce to nearest-neighbor
classification into $200$ buckets. We tested this directly: for the identical noisy queries
used above, a raw-pixel Euclidean nearest-neighbor classifier among the $M$ stored clean images
predicts which grid is present, and we compared its exact-match rate against ours with a paired
McNemar test. The answer is not reassuring: nearest-neighbor performs \emph{at least as well,
and significantly better}, across nearly the entire informative range. At $\sigma{=}0.40$,
MNIST nearest-neighbor is still at $100\%$ while our pipeline is at $82.0\%$ (McNemar
$p<0.0001$); at $\sigma{=}1.00$ nearest-neighbor remains at $95.0\%$ while ours has fallen to
$4.0\%$. The pattern replicates on Fashion-MNIST and CIFAR-100 (Table~\ref{tab:hetero-datasets}
vs.\ the corresponding nearest-neighbor rates, all $p<0.0001$ except two borderline points at
the highest tested noise). At $M{=}200$ with well-separated exemplars, Euclidean
nearest-neighbor is close to the likelihood-optimal classifier under additive Gaussian
corruption; routing through a lossy, sparsely-thresholded engram throws away information the
raw pixels still carried. We report this plainly rather than omit it: whatever this
architecture's value, it is not yet raw classification accuracy at small $M$. Its case has to
rest on properties nearest-neighbor search structurally lacks -- a fixed-size, compressed,
content-addressable code that never revisits the stored exemplars, and (Section~\ref{sec:hop})
a qualitatively different failure mode under scale than classical attractor memories.

\subsection{Capacity: two independent ceilings, one of them fixable}
\label{sec:capacity-hetero}

Scaling $M$ from 200 to 2000 (fixed $H{=}1000$, default ridge $\lambda{=}10^{-4}$) reproduces
what looks, at first, like a hard cliff: exact recovery at $\sigma{=}0.1$ falls from $100\%$
($M{\leq}300$) to $8.3\%$ at $M{=}500$. Measuring the image-side Gram matrix directly
($X X^\top$, $X$ the $M{\times}D$ image matrix) explains why: its condition number grows from
$1.8{\times}10^4$ ($M{=}200$) to $3.6{\times}10^7$ ($M{=}500$), crossing the fixed ridge value
exactly where the collapse occurs. Re-solving with $\lambda{=}0.1$ at $M{=}500$ restores
$100\%$ exact recovery at $\sigma{=}0.1$ while costing almost nothing in zero-noise fidelity
($0.994$ vs.\ $1.000$ overlap) -- this specific cliff is a stale hyperparameter, not an
architectural limit.

A second, genuinely structural ceiling remains underneath it. The image Gram matrix's
\emph{effective rank} -- not its nominal pixel dimension $D{=}784$ -- caps exact zero-noise
fitting: for this MNIST sample it saturates at ${\sim}600$--$610$ regardless of how large $M$
grows (rank $568/800$ at $M{=}800$, only reaching $607/2000$ by $M{=}2000$), a property of the
data's intrinsic dimensionality that no amount of $H$ or ridge tuning can lift. CIFAR-100
grayscale, by contrast, fills its \emph{entire} ambient dimension (rank $1024/1024$ by
$M{=}1600$) -- natural texture carries more independent information per pixel than a clean
digit stroke on a black background, though this does not translate into better absolute
recovery (Table~\ref{tab:hetero-datasets}). A separate, $H$-dependent ceiling governs the
\emph{reverse} (engram$\to$image) direction: its Gram matrix's rank is bounded by
$\min(M,H)$, and doubling $H$ from $1000$ to $2000$ (density held fixed) measurably delays this
one -- exact recovery at $M{=}2000$, $\sigma{=}0.1$ rises from $80\%$ ($H{=}1000$) to $90\%$
($H{=}2000$) -- with zero effect on the image-side ceiling, exactly as the algebra predicts.

with $M$? We extended the sweep to $M{=}2500$--$4000$ across $\lambda\in\{1,10,50,100\}$:

\subsection{Comparison to classical Hopfield networks}
\label{sec:hop}

Vector-HaSH's stated motivation is avoiding ``the memory cliff of prior memory models''
\cite{chandra2025}. We tested this claim directly rather than citing it, building four
auto-associative memories on the \emph{same} bipolarized MNIST images and comparing them
against our full heteroassociative-plus-cleanup pipeline: (i) the classical outer-product
(Hebbian) rule; (ii) a mean-centered covariance rule correcting for population-level pixel
bias; (iii) the pseudo-inverse (projection) rule -- the same learning-rule family our own
network uses, but with no separate error-correcting scaffold; and (iv) our network. As a
sanity check on the classical rule's textbook behavior, we first reproduced its capacity cliff
on \emph{random} bipolar patterns ($N{=}784$): exact recovery falls from $82.5\%$ ($M{=}60$)
to $0\%$ ($M{=}120$), matching the analytic prediction ($M/N\approx0.138$) almost exactly.

On real, correlated images the picture is markedly worse for every auto-associative variant.
The classical rule fails even at \emph{zero} noise, for any $M$ we tested: a clean cue's
overlap with itself drops from $1.0$ to $0.84$ after a single update, because $86\%$ of MNIST
pixels are background and the plain Hebbian rule's dominant mode locks onto that population
bias rather than any specific image. Mean-centering removes this bias exactly as intended --
we verified it recovers correctly at $M{\leq}3$ -- but real images correlate with each other
far beyond a first-moment bias, and this corrected rule still collapses to $0\%$ by $M{=}10$,
two orders of magnitude below the random-pattern prediction. The pseudo-inverse rule is the
most instructive comparison, because it is the same rule ours uses: under raw random bit-flips
it is algebraically \emph{exact} at zero noise up to at least $M{=}200$, but its basins of
attraction shrink so fast under even modest noise that $5\%$ bit-flip recall falls from
$100\%$ ($M{=}100$) to $25\%$ ($M{=}150$) to $0\%$ ($M{=}200$).

The comparison above used two different corruption models -- bit-flips for the three
auto-associative baselines, Gaussian pixel noise for ours -- with no shared severity axis. We
redid it with a single, shared model: every method sees the same Gaussian-corrupted continuous
image per trial, and the three Hopfield variants receive it bipolarized (thresholded at
$0.5$), so their bit-flips arise naturally from the same noise process rather than being
imposed separately. At $n{=}200$/point (matching Table~\ref{tab:hetero-datasets}'s rigor):

The capacity-scaling story is unchanged, and now more sharply confirmed: pseudo-inverse

network and the pseudo-inverse baseline are statistically comparable across the entire noise
range, and the pseudo-inverse rule is marginally \emph{ahead} at the highest noise tested
($74.5\%$ vs.\ $71.5\%$ at $\sigma{=}1.0$). Our network's real advantage over the identical
learning rule is not noise tolerance at fixed $M$ -- on that axis they are essentially tied --
it is capacity scaling: the two share equal footing until $M$ crosses somewhere between $100$
and $200$, past which only the version with a separate error-correcting attractor survives.
This is a narrower and more precise claim than a blanket ``more robust'' would have been, and it
is the one the shared-noise-model data actually supports.

Because the pseudo-inverse baseline uses the identical learning rule to ours, this isolates
the source of the capacity difference precisely: not the pseudo-inverse rule itself, but the
presence of a separate, content-specific error-correcting step (the Sudoku-legality attractor)
layered on top of it. This is the piece Vector-HaSH's true scaffold provides in a
content-\emph{independent} way (Section~2); ours is content-dependent (legality-specific) but
still delivers the qualitative signature the scaffold is meant to produce, and does so an
order of magnitude beyond where the shared learning rule alone can reach.

\subsection{Comparison to Modern Hopfield, sparse distributed memory, and dense associative
memory: capacity as a property of the key space, not the neuron count}
\label{sec:modern-comparison}

Section~\ref{sec:hop}'s conclusion -- that our advantage over classical/pseudo-inverse Hopfield
is capacity scaling, not noise tolerance -- raises an obvious next question: does that
advantage hold against the broader associative-memory literature, or only against the specific
three variants tested? Gershman, Fiete \& Irie's unification (already tested at the level of a
single swapped nonlinearity in Section~\ref{sec:separation}) names three further architectures
we had not yet built as complete, independently-constructed systems: Modern (continuous)
Hopfield networks \cite{ramsauer2020}, sparse distributed memory \cite{kanerva1988}, and dense
associative memory \cite{krotov2016}. Unlike Section~\ref{sec:separation}'s test, which swapped
a nonlinearity inside \emph{our own} encoder, this comparison builds each architecture with its
own native representation -- critically, Modern Hopfield and dense associative memory operate
directly on the full, uncompressed image, with no sparse engram bottleneck at all -- so it
tests something Section~\ref{sec:separation} could not: whether compressing into a fixed
$H$-dimensional sparse code costs anything relative to a full continuous or dense store at
matched stored-item count.

\textbf{Construction.} Table~\ref{tab:construction} gives unit and parameter counts for all
seven methods at $M{=}200$. Modern Hopfield and dense associative memory need no trained weight
matrix at all -- just the $M\times784$ pattern matrix itself, smaller than the three Hopfield
variants' fixed $784\times784$ weight matrix once $M<784$. Sparse distributed memory uses
$1000$ fixed random ``hard locations'' (matched to our $H{=}1000$ for a resource-comparable
test). Our network is the only architecture whose stored representation ($H{=}1000$-dimensional
sparse engrams) is decoupled from the content's own dimension ($784$ for MNIST): the same fixed
$W_{SH}$ and the same class of engram serves this task, the three other image datasets of
Section~\ref{sec:generalization}, and the original Sudoku-legality task of Sections 2--3,
whereas every other method's stored representation \emph{is} the content and must be rebuilt
per domain.

values that happened to work, following this project's standing practice of surfacing
parameter-sensitivity issues rather than absorbing them into an unstated default

\textbf{What this means for the architecture's value proposition.} Modern Hopfield and dense
associative memory's stored representation \emph{is} the image itself: their capacity is a
direct function of the content's own dimension, exactly the ``neuron-number-limits-capacity''
coupling classical associative-memory theory assumes. Our architecture instead separates the
\emph{key} (a sparse engram drawn from an astronomically large combinatorial space -- valid
$9\times9$ Sudoku grids number on the order of $6.7\times10^{21}$ -- entirely independent of
the content's own representation) from the \emph{value} (here, an arbitrary image, but Section~
\ref{sec:generalization} shows the same key works for three unrelated content types, and
Sections 2--3 show it simultaneously supports the Sudoku-legality task itself). This decoupling
is what Section~\ref{sec:generalization} demonstrates across datasets and what this section
prices honestly: at matched, currently-modest engram size, we pay for that decoupling in raw
noise tolerance at high $M$ relative to two architectures whose entire representation is
dedicated to exactly this one task. The two are not in conflict -- a single fixed key
infrastructure serving arbitrary content and simultaneously solving a hard combinatorial
constraint problem is a different kind of claim than ``best available image-noise-recovery
system,'' and Table~\ref{tab:construction} shows why the two are not free to compare on raw
accuracy alone: three of the six alternatives (all but pseudo-inverse Hopfield) have no
mechanism whatsoever for the Sudoku-completion task this same engram also performs. Where the
two claims overlap -- recovery accuracy at low-to-moderate noise -- our architecture already
matches these two purpose-built alternatives (Figure~\ref{fig:modern-comparison-highM}, right
panel, $\sigma{\leq}0.2$), which is the regime relevant if downstream error correction is
handled separately, as it is in this architecture (see the Discussion for how this connects to
the emergent spiking mechanism of Sections~\ref{sec:sync}--\ref{sec:emergent}).

One further, important scoping note: the cleanup step used throughout this section and
Section~\ref{sec:hop} is the \emph{symbolic} conflict-mask attractor of Section~2.1, not the
spiking network of Sections~\ref{sec:sync}--\ref{sec:emergent}. The two are shown elsewhere in
this paper to be statistically indistinguishable on the core Sudoku-completion task
(Table~\ref{tab:wilcoxon}), but that equivalence has not been separately re-verified for the
image-heteroassociation pipeline specifically, and this comparison should not be read as a test
of the spiking mechanism.

\subsection{A modular, Vector-HaSH-style scaffold: does the actual addressing mechanism help?}
\label{sec:vectorhash-scaffold}

Section~\ref{sec:hetero} borrows Vector-HaSH's bidirectional pseudo-inverse heteroassociation
rule but keeps our own flat, non-modular scaffold ($W_{SH}$ random projection $+$ $k$-WTA) for
the key. Vector-HaSH's own defining mechanism is different: several small, pairwise-coprime
periodic modules (grid-cell-like), each contributing a one-hot code for its own residue of a
stored item's index, so the combined code addresses a space of size $\prod_i p_i$ using only
$\sum_i p_i$ total units. We test this mechanism directly rather than only its heteroassociation
rule: $10$ modules with prime periods $97$--$139$ ($\sum p_i=1164$, comparable to our own
$H{=}1000$; $\prod p_i\approx4.2\times10^{20}$), each stored item's code fixed by its index via
the Chinese Remainder Theorem, with a pseudo-inverse encoder trained to map the raw image to
this code -- the direct analogue of $W_{SI}$ in Section~\ref{sec:hetero}.

\textbf{Cleanup must be recurrent, not independent.} A naive cleanup -- snap each module's
continuous estimate to its own nearest phase, independently -- collapses far faster with $M$
than every other method in Table~\ref{tab:construction}: $7.5\%$ at $M{=}800$, $\sigma{=}0.3$
(vs.\ $57$--$99\%$ for everything else), because success requires all $10$ independent decisions
to be simultaneously correct, with no mechanism for a confident module to correct a noisy one.
Replacing this with a genuine recurrent step -- a Hopfield-style softmax relaxation (the same
mathematical form as Modern Hopfield above, $\beta$ re-tuned to $100$ on this task by the
identical zero-noise-recovery discipline used throughout this section) over the space of the $M$
\emph{known valid} codes, so every module's evidence can inform every other module's correction
-- changes the picture entirely:

\textbf{Where the corrected mechanism lands relative to everything else.} With recurrence, the
modular scaffold outperforms our own architecture at every $M{\geq}200$ tested, by a wide margin
at $M{=}800$ ($81.0\%$ vs.\ our $57.5\%$), while still trailing the two sharpest, purpose-built

($96\%$). Also reconciled to this exact $\sigma{=}0.3$ condition, raw-pixel $k$-NN is at
$100\%$ throughout $M{=}100$--$800$: the ranking is $k\text{-NN}\approx$ Modern Hopfield
$\approx$ DAM $>$ modular scaffold $>$ ours $\gg$ pseudo-inverse Hopfield $\gg$
classical/covariance Hopfield/SDM.

\textbf{Does image dimensionality limit capacity here?} Not in the way it limits the other
methods, but not in the way ``escapes the neuron-count constraint'' would suggest either. The
mechanism separates two distinct jobs. \emph{Discrimination} -- identifying which of the $M$
items a cue belongs to -- happens entirely in the abstract code space, whose size ($\prod p_i$)
is fixed by our choice of moduli and has no dependence on image content at all. \emph{Content
mapping} -- translating between a code and actual pixels, via the pseudo-inverse encoder --
depends on image dimensionality exactly as it does for our own $W_{SI}$: fitting $M$ patterns
requires inverting an $M\times M$ Gram matrix built from the raw images, which becomes
ill-conditioned once $M$ approaches the images' \emph{effective rank}, not their nominal
dimension. We verified this directly on the same $M{=}800$ image set
(\texttt{vectorhash\_modular\_scaffold\_gram\_analysis.py}): the Gram matrix's
condition number grows from $3.0\times10^3$ ($M{=}100$) to $2.8\times10^{12}$ ($M{=}800$), and
the effective rank (99.9\% energy) is only $387$ at $M{=}800$ -- below $M$ itself -- while the
code space itself ($4.2\times10^{20}$) is nowhere near taxed. The decline from $98.0\%$
($M{=}400$) to $81.0\%$ ($M{=}800$) is this same content-mapping ceiling reasserting itself, not
a limitation of the modular addressing idea. The two capacity questions are genuinely separate,
and modularity solves only the first.

\textbf{Storage and time complexity: a fixed weight matrix vs.\ an explicit, growing list.}
Table~\ref{tab:complexity} makes the comparison precise. Classical/covariance/pseudo-inverse
Hopfield compress everything into one $D{\times}D$ weight matrix: storage and per-query time are
independent of $M$, which is exactly why they hit a hard capacity ceiling -- a fixed-size store
cannot hold unboundedly many patterns without interference. Modern Hopfield, dense associative
memory, $k$-NN, and (for its growing engram list) our own architecture instead keep an explicit,
uncompressed-or-lightly-compressed store that grows linearly with $M$ -- the same complexity
class, not a more efficient one, which is why none of them shows a hard cliff. The modular
scaffold sits in this second group for query time (it still scores the cue against all $M$
known codes), but its \emph{storage} has a genuine structural advantage none of the others share:
because each code is generated deterministically from its item's index via $m\bmod p_i$, nothing
needs to be stored beyond the index itself -- $O(M)$ integers, rather than $O(MD)$ raw patterns
or $O(MH)$ engrams.

The honest summary: neither our architecture nor the modular scaffold escapes the $O(M)$
query-time growth that motivated this whole comparison -- both still compare a cue against every
stored item, exactly as $k$-NN, Modern Hopfield, and dense associative memory do. What
Vector-HaSH's actual mechanism contributes beyond our flat scaffold is a smaller, structurally
guaranteed storage constant, and a discrimination capacity that is provably decoupled from
content dimensionality -- not a different complexity class for the content-mapping problem, which
remains bounded by the same effective-rank ceiling for every method that must fit a linear map to
real images.

\section{Discussion}

Three results compose into a single picture. The combinatorial engram code is robust across
a 200-fold range of storage load and independent of connection density
(Table~\ref{tab:collapse}); the attention-based key-value readout built on top of it shows no
observed capacity ceiling where a regression-based readout collapses catastrophically
(Table~\ref{tab:readout}); and the symbolic legality constraint this architecture relies on
for cleanup can be realized as an emergent property of a spiking network's phase-synchrony
dynamics, statistically matching -- not exceeding -- the hard-coded rule
(Table~\ref{tab:wilcoxon}). We regard the ``matches, not exceeds'' framing as the honest and
more interesting finding: it demonstrates that a biologically-plausible, locally-wired
dynamical mechanism can substitute for a piece of hard-coded logic without loss, which is a
different and, we think, more useful claim than a performance improvement would have been.

Two further results test claims we had, until this paper, only argued for by analogy rather
than measured directly. First, the encoder itself behaves as a similarity-preserving hash, not
merely a randomizing sparsifier: engram overlap tracks raw coincidence-matrix similarity almost
monotonically (Spearman $\rho=0.974$, Section~\ref{sec:hashing}), the property the fly
olfactory hashing circuit \cite{dasgupta2017} is known to provide and that every graceful-degradation
curve in this paper implicitly assumes but does not itself demonstrate. Second, unifying
correlation-matrix memory, sparse distributed memory, dense associative memory, and
Transformer attention as one computation differing only in its separation operator
\cite{gershman2025} is not just a re-description after the fact -- swapping that one
nonlinearity in our own readout changes noise robustness by tens of percentage points
(Section~\ref{sec:separation}), and the mechanism responsible (gap-based vs.\ ratio-based
sharpening under a non-negligible interference floor) is a genuine refinement of, not merely a
restatement of, that unification.

A theme recurs across every result in this paper: failure, when it happens, is graceful
rather than catastrophic. The combinatorial code and its attention readout hold up to
$30$--$35\%$ query corruption before collapsing over a narrow window rather than failing
sharply (Section~\ref{sec:graceful-noise}); partial-grid completion degrades smoothly with
clue count instead of failing at a hard threshold (Table~\ref{tab:completion}); and the
spiking mechanism tolerates substantial drive-current noise, remaining a net improvement over
the symbolic guess even when corrupted well past the spacing between adjacent digit levels
(Section~\ref{sec:drive-noise}). Where a cleanup pass exists, its benefit is not uniform but
concentrates specifically in the transition region -- the conflict-mask cleanup's rescue
effect on query noise peaks sharply at $42\%$ (Figure~\ref{fig:feedback-noise}, right) --
exactly where ambiguity is highest and correction is most needed, and negligible everywhere
else, either because the query was already easy or already unrecoverable. The cross-modal
extension (Section~\ref{sec:hetero}) reinforces this theme with an external, hard baseline:
on identical real images, three classical/pseudo-inverse auto-associative memories all
collapse to exactly $0\%$ recovery within a narrow range of stored-item count, while our
architecture -- sharing the pseudo-inverse learning rule with one of them, but adding a
separate legality-based cleanup step -- degrades smoothly and remains above $0\%$ an order of
magnitude further out (Table~\ref{tab:hopfield}). This is the same qualitative signature
Vector-HaSH's content-independent scaffold is built to produce, obtained here from a
content-\emph{dependent} one.

We invoke the statistical mechanics of constraint satisfaction and random $k$-SAT
\cite{mezard2002,mezard2009} as motivating context for why constraint density and dynamics
near a solvability boundary are worth studying together -- and, relatedly, why McGuire et
al.'s proof that no 16-clue Sudoku has a unique solution \cite{mcguire2012} is a natural
reference point for combinatorial threshold phenomena in this domain -- but we do not claim
to extend that literature's universality results. Section~\ref{sec:crossover}'s honestly
reported non-universal exponent is the reason for this restraint, and we consider explicitly
reporting a negative/mixed finding here preferable to omitting it.

\textbf{Limitations.} The architecture's shared, common oscillatory drive is structurally
incompatible with anti-phase-based constraint encoding (Section~\ref{sec:sync}); this is a
property of this specific circuit, not a general claim about spiking constraint satisfaction.
The low-clue-fraction regime remains limited by coincidence-matrix specificity, and we have
not tested whether a smarter (e.g., adaptive) synaptic mechanism could improve it without
compromising the clean matching result of Table~\ref{tab:wilcoxon} -- we deliberately did not
pursue synaptic plasticity in the reported architecture, since the non-plastic, fixed-weight
dynamics already achieve parity with the symbolic rule, and we judged added mechanism
without added validated benefit to be the wrong trade for this paper.

The cross-modal extension has its own, separately reported limitations, three of which we
tested directly rather than leaving open. First, it is not yet more accurate than a trivial
raw-pixel nearest-neighbor classifier at $M{=}200$ (Section~\ref{sec:knn-baseline}), and we
checked whether this improves at the large-$M$ scale where a fixed-size engram's storage
advantage should start to matter -- it does not: the gap widens from $+3.5$ percentage points
at $M{=}200$ to $+96.0$ points at $M{=}2000$ (Table~\ref{tab:knn-vs-M}), since nearest-neighbor
accuracy is nearly insensitive to $M$ while ours is not. Whatever this architecture is for, it
is not for beating nearest-neighbor lookup at any $M$ we tested; its case would have to rest
on properties outside classification accuracy entirely (e.g.\ fusing partial pixel and partial
relational cues, which nearest-neighbor over images alone cannot do). Second, the
ridge-regularization question is resolved rather than open: extending the sweep to
$M{=}4000$ (Table~\ref{tab:ridge-extended}) shows the required $\lambda$ does \emph{not} keep
growing -- a single $\lambda{\approx}10$ stays near-optimal throughout -- and the residual
decline past $M{=}2000$ is fully attributable to the already-identified image effective-rank
ceiling, not to unresolved regularization. Third, the Hopfield comparison's mismatched noise
models (bit-flip vs.\ Gaussian) have been replaced with a single shared model
(Section~\ref{sec:hop}), which revealed a more precise finding than the original qualitative
one: at fixed, small $M$ our network and the pseudo-inverse baseline are statistically
comparable in noise tolerance -- the real advantage is capacity scaling, not noise robustness
per se. That advantage, however, does not generalize to the broader associative-memory
literature: extending the comparison to Modern Hopfield networks and dense associative memory
(Section~\ref{sec:modern-comparison}), both given properly re-validated separation sharpness
rather than naively carried-over defaults, shows both substantially outperform our raw
recovery accuracy at high $M$ under matched noise ($99.0\%$ and $96.0\%$ vs.\ our $57.5\%$ at
$M{=}800$, $\sigma{=}0.3$), a gap that a $3\times$ larger engram narrows by about half but does
not close. We report this as a genuine limitation, not a caveat to explain away: this
architecture's case cannot rest on being the best available image-associative memory. It rests
instead on a structural property neither alternative has -- the key (a sparse engram drawn from
an astronomically large combinatorial space, decoupled from the content's own representation)
is entirely separate from the value (arbitrary content, here images, but Section~
\ref{sec:generalization} shows three unrelated content types share the identical key
infrastructure, which simultaneously performs the Sudoku-legality task of Sections 2--3). Where
the two kinds of claim actually overlap -- recovery at low-to-moderate noise -- our architecture
already matches both purpose-built alternatives.

\textbf{Two kinds of error correction.} Every alternative memory tested in
Sections~\ref{sec:hop}--\ref{sec:modern-comparison} corrects errors by an abstract mathematical
procedure: classical/covariance/pseudo-inverse Hopfield and dense associative memory descend an
energy function via synchronous sign updates; Modern Hopfield iterates a softmax fixed-point
map; a symbolic Sudoku solver would backtrack over candidate assignments. None of these is a
physical process -- each requires a global objective (an energy, a normalized similarity
distribution, a search tree) that the update rule has privileged access to. Sections~
\ref{sec:sync}--\ref{sec:emergent} of this paper test a genuinely different kind of mechanism
for the same job: a network of leaky-integrate-and-fire neurons, coupled only through local
excitatory/inhibitory synapses with no encoded objective function at all, whose relaxation to a
phase-synchronized steady state realizes the identical legality-checking computation --
verified statistically indistinguishable from the hard-coded rule it replaces
(Table~\ref{tab:wilcoxon}). This is not a claim we extend to the image-heteroassociation
comparison above, where the cleanup step is the symbolic conflict-mask, not the spiking network
(Section~\ref{sec:modern-comparison} notes this explicitly) -- but it is a validated property of
this same architecture's core task, and one none of the six alternative memories in
Sections~\ref{sec:hop}--\ref{sec:modern-comparison} attempt in any form.

\textbf{The scaffold, not just the learning rule, is a free choice.} Section~
\ref{sec:vectorhash-scaffold} tests Vector-HaSH's actual modular addressing mechanism, not just
the heteroassociation rule borrowed from it, and finds it beats our own flat scaffold once given
a genuinely recurrent (not independent) cleanup step -- an instructive result against our own
architecture, obtained by taking a cited comparison seriously enough to build rather than only
invoke. A complexity analysis sharpens what this actually buys: neither the modular scaffold nor
our own engram escapes $O(M)$ query-time growth, the same class $k$-NN and the sharp associative
memories of Section~\ref{sec:modern-comparison} already occupy -- the real, structural advantage
modularity contributes is a discrimination capacity provably decoupled from content
dimensionality, plus an $O(M)$-index storage cost rather than an $O(MD)$ or $O(MH)$ one. Content
fidelity itself remains bounded by the same image effective-rank ceiling as everything else.
We view this as strengthening, not undercutting, the honest accounting in Section~
\ref{sec:modern-comparison}: this architecture's case was never that it is the most efficient or
most accurate available memory, but that its specific combination of properties -- a fixed key
infrastructure serving arbitrary content, a combinatorially large native address space, and (on
its original task) a physically-realizable dynamical error-correction mechanism -- is not
available from any single alternative tested.

\section{Methods}

All simulations use Brian2 \cite{brian2}. Spiking network parameters: $\tau_m=40$\,ms,
$f_{\mathrm{osc}}=10$\,Hz, $V_{\mathrm{th}}=1$, $V_{\mathrm{reset}}=0$, integration timestep
$0.1$\,ms (RK4), $w_{IE}=0.005$, $w_{EE}=0.001$ (Section~\ref{sec:sync} operating point).
Digit-encoding drive currents use nine evenly spaced levels chosen so every level reliably
crosses threshold at least once per oscillation cycle (below ${\sim}0.46$, a level never
fires; above this floor, separation in first-spike phase is monotonic in current). Simulation
window 3000\,ms per trial, with the first 1500\,ms discarded as transient; every trial builds
a fresh network instance (Brian2's absolute simulation clock does not reset on a reused
network object, so a persistent network across trials would confound successive trials'
oscillation phase). Initial membrane potential is randomized per trial
($\mathrm{Uniform}(0,0.1) + \mathrm{Uniform}(-0.1,0.1)$ perturbation).

Engram/readout parameters: $H=1000$, $K=150$ (capacity/robustness sweeps) or $K=400$
(crossover characterization, chosen as the sharpest transition in an earlier $M$--$K$ grid
search), projection density $\rho=0.02$, softmax temperature $\tau=2.0\times(K/150)$ (scaled
to the engram's self-overlap). Statistical tests: two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank
(\texttt{scipy.stats.wilcoxon}) on paired per-trial accuracy differences, identical target
grid and known-cell mask across compared methods within each trial.

Heteroassociation (Section~\ref{sec:hetero}): $W_{SI}$/$W_{IS}$ trained via the dual-form
ridge pseudo-inverse ($\lambda=10^{-4}$ default, $\lambda=0.1$--$10$ where noted for
larger $M$, verified stable up to $M{=}4000$), images normalized to $[0,1]$ and Gaussian
noise added and reclipped to that range. MNIST/Fashion-MNIST are $28\times28$ (dimension
$784$); CIFAR-100 is converted to grayscale by averaging RGB channels ($32\times32$,
dimension $1024$). Baseline (Section~\ref{sec:knn-baseline}): paired McNemar exact test
(\texttt{scipy.stats.binomtest} on discordant-pair counts) between our pipeline's and
nearest-neighbor's exact-match outcome on identical noisy queries, at $M{=}200$ and,
separately, across $M{=}200$--$2000$ at fixed $\sigma{=}0.4$. Hopfield baselines (Section~
\ref{sec:hop}): images bipolarized by thresholding at $0.5$; classical rule is the
outer-product/Hebbian sum with zero diagonal; covariance rule subtracts each pixel's own mean
activity (not a single global scalar) before the same sum; pseudo-inverse rule reuses the
identical dual-form solver as $W_{SI}$/$W_{IS}$ with the pattern set as both input and target.
Synchronous recall update $s\leftarrow\mathrm{sign}(Ws)$ preserves a neuron's current state on
an exact tie (net input $0$) rather than forcing it to $+1$, since mean-centered patterns can
have exactly-zero weights on pixels that are constant across every stored image. The
headline Hopfield comparison (Table~\ref{tab:hopfield}) uses a single shared noise model
across all four methods: a Gaussian-corrupted continuous image is generated once per trial,
consumed directly by our network and bipolarized (thresholded at $0.5$) for the three
Hopfield variants, at $n{=}200$/point with Wilson 95\% CIs; an earlier bit-flip-vs-Gaussian
mismatched version is reported separately in the text as a mechanism illustration only.

Modern Hopfield/SDM/DAM comparison (Section~\ref{sec:modern-comparison}): Modern Hopfield
patterns are $L_2$-normalized (both stored patterns and the query) before the softmax update
$\xi\leftarrow X^\top\mathrm{softmax}(\beta X\xi)$, iterated to a fixed point ($\max20$
iterations, $\beta{=}60$); success is nearest-stored-pattern correctness by Euclidean distance,
not exact numerical equality, since a converged continuous state need not match bit-for-bit.
Dense associative memory uses the synchronous update $s\leftarrow\mathrm{sign}(\sum_\mu n\cdot
\mathrm{ReLU}(s\cdot x_\mu/d)^{n-1}x_\mu)$, $n{=}60$, iterated to convergence (max $30$
iterations). Sparse distributed memory uses $L{=}1000$ random bipolar hard locations and a
Hamming radius chosen so a pair of independent random bipolar vectors of the stored dimension
($d{=}784$) falls within it with probability $0.02$ (via the binomial quantile function);
writing accumulates the full stored pattern into every hard location within radius, reading
sums the counters of every activated hard location and thresholds by sign. All three methods'
sharpening parameters ($\beta$, $n$, and SDM's target activation fraction) were validated by
checking zero-noise self-recovery across the full $M{=}100$--$800$ sweep before any
noise-robustness number was computed (Section~\ref{sec:modern-comparison}); naive carryovers
from other contexts in this paper ($\beta{=}0.5$, $n{=}8$) fail this check almost completely.
The $H$-scaling test (Table~\ref{tab:H-scaling-comparison}) reuses the identical pipeline as
Section~\ref{sec:capacity-hetero} with $H$ varied and $K{=}0.15H$ held fixed throughout.
